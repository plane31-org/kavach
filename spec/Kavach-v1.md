# Kavach v1

End-to-end encrypted, relayed, durable records between a person's devices and the devices of the people they share with, for circles of up to 100 accounts, through a server that stores only what it cannot open and deletes each record once every reader has it. What a record means is the product's; the protocol carries bytes of a kind the product declares.

Kavach v1, protocol revision 9, formerly SealedStream SS5. Wire version 5, unchanged since revision 2; revision 6 assigned a flag bit that was reserved, revision 7 changed no byte, revision 8 puts an envelope in front of every record payload, and revision 9 adds one document type, the account succession of amendment A1 (`spec/amendments/A1-authority-replacement.md`, adopted), without changing any existing byte. November 2026. An implementation profile states a product's record kinds, payload codecs, platform, server, retention and replacement-model choices; `spec/Profile-template.md` lists what a profile must decide, and each product keeps its profile in its own repository. Proposed amendments under review live in `spec/amendments/`; adopted ones are folded into this text and kept there for their history.

### 1. Scope and requirements

Kavach carries a person's records, of whatever kinds the product declares (a position, an event, a state), to a small circle of people they chose, through a server that cannot read any of it. One account is one person. Each account has one active device, keeps its memberships when the device changes, and can own circles. The server authenticates accounts, stores opaque documents and ciphertext, forwards records, and deletes each record as soon as every recipient has acknowledged it.

This document is normative for Kavach v1. MUST and MUST NOT are requirements, SHOULD is a strong recommendation, MAY is optional. It is an implementation proposal, not an independently audited protocol. The machine-readable vectors described in section 16 travel with it.

#### Requirements

| Requirement | How the protocol meets it |
| --- | --- |
| Membership belongs to the account | A circle lists stable account identities, never devices. The identity key survives a device replacement, so memberships and ownership do. |
| Owner continuity | The owner keeps the same account key and circle id on a new device. Replacing the owner's device never closes a circle or removes a member. |
| A new device keeps its circles, not its keys | Each device has its own encryption key. A replacement device recovers the account, publishes a new certificate, and receives new epoch keys as each author syncs. Membership continuity is guaranteed; an instant view is not. Replacement is also what cuts the old device off. |
| Owner approval only | A participant requests admission; the owner approves that account once. No invitation secret, no other-member signatures, no second ceremony. |
| History lives on devices | Devices keep what they received under the host's retention policy. A replacement device never imports old keys or history. The server keeps a record only until every recipient has it. |
| Recovery first, reset last | A new device recovers the account through a platform recovery provider or a saved recovery code. An explicit destructive reset exists for the case where both fail. |
| Kinds are the product's | A record carries a kind the product declares, with the behaviour it declares: how long a reader keeps it, whether a newer queued one replaces it, whether it is kept in history, whether one newest-per-author slot is kept for it, and what the relay may see of it in the header. The protocol gives a kind no meaning and never reads a payload. |
| Durable events and current state | A kind declared with history and no slot keeps every record under its own id; nothing overwrites it, and the host may notify recipients of it visibly by its header class. A kind declared with a slot keeps the newest per author from the author's current device, ordered by the event time the sender gives; a kind declared latest-wins yields in the queue to a newer one. |
| Client-only SDK | The SDK owns cryptography and local security state. The host supplies authenticated login, a directory, an opaque relay, notifications and the interface. The SDK ships no server. |

#### Fixed limits

A circle has 1 to 100 accounts including its owner. An author's effective audience is the union of its enabled circles plus itself, capped at 100 distinct accounts; an over-limit change is rejected or pauses sharing with `RECIPIENT_LIMIT`, never silently truncated. An account vault holds at most 100 circle entries. `accountName` is the host's immutable account identifier, 1 to 64 ASCII letters, digits, underscore or hyphen, case-sensitive.

Device generations, circle revisions, vault revisions and reset-key list versions are u64 starting at 1. Epochs are u32 starting at 1; record counters are u32 starting at 0. Nothing wraps: counter exhaustion rotates the epoch, epoch exhaustion starts a new device stream, revision or generation exhaustion is a terminal maintenance error. An author rotates on any known authorization change, on counter exhaustion, on clock reversal, and when its epoch is older than the profile's hygiene interval. Every document is at most 16,384 bytes; a plaintext payload is at most 4,096 bytes.

#### Out of scope

Multiple simultaneous devices per account, delegated owners, ownership transfer, reusable invites, peer key fulfilment, manifest pruning, ratchets, forward secrecy, post-compromise recovery, algorithm negotiation, guaranteed alert delivery and history migration to a new device. Section 15 names each where it affects a security claim.

### 2. Terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Account | A person as the host knows them: an immutable `accountName` plus the Ed25519 account key `AK` generated on their first device. The identity id is derived from both; the account id is the identity id at enrollment and never changes. |
| Identity id | `H(C("identity") || name || AK)`: the identity of one account key. It changes when the key changes through a succession. |
| Account id | The identity id the account enrolled under. Circles, manifests, streams and records name accounts by it; a succession moves the key and leaves it unchanged. |
| Succession | An AccountSuccession (type 9) signed by a host authority key the key list allows: it binds an account id to a new account key and certificate at the next generation, after the product's fresh authentication. Its ordered list from the account id to the current key is the account's chain. |
| Replacement model | How a new device proves it is the account: identity continuity (it holds the account key) or account recovery (the host's authority signs a succession). A profile enables one or both; peers enforce the choice. |
| Device | The one active device of an account. It holds a device seed from which its signing key `DSK` and its encryption key `DEK` derive, a random 16-byte `deviceId`, and a generation that rises by one at each replacement. Neither key derives from the account. |
| Certificate | The AccountDevice document (type 1): the account's current device generation and public keys, signed by `AK`. Its body hash is the `certificateHash` that manifests bind to. |
| Circle | A named group with one owner. Its membership is one owner-signed snapshot (type 2) listing stable identity ids. |
| Join request | Host state: an authenticated account asking to be admitted to a circle, or to leave it. It carries no signature. Only the owner's next snapshot changes membership. |
| Vault | The encrypted AccountVault (type 6): the account seed plus circle pins and sharing preferences, stored on the host under the recovery key. It holds no encryption key. |
| Stream | One author device's sequence of records; its id derives from the identity id and the device id. |
| Epoch | A span of an author's stream encrypted under one random key `SK`. |
| Manifest | The signed EpochManifest (type 3): the epoch's audience as (identity, certificate hash, wrap hash) rows plus a key commitment. |
| Wrap | A libsodium sealed box of `SK` to one recipient device's `DEK`: 80 bytes, unsigned, bound to the manifest by its hash. |
| Record | An EncryptedRecord (type 4): one record of a product-declared kind, encrypted under `SK`, signed by `DSK`. |
| Audience | The identities an author encrypts to: itself plus every member of every circle it has joined and enabled, each at its current device. |
| Desired revision | A local u64 the author increments on every change that should alter its audience. An epoch prepared under an older value is obsolete. |
| Delivery window | The profile's configured time after the relay accepted a record during which it keeps it for readers that have not acknowledged it. After it, the record is deleted whether or not it was delivered. |
| Cursor | One u64 per reader per author stream, held by the relay: every record at or below it is acknowledged by that reader. The relay deletes a record once every reader the epoch addresses has a cursor past it. |
| Host | The server operator: authenticates accounts, stores opaque documents and ciphertext, forwards records, deletes them once delivered. |
| Reset receipt | An AccountResetReceipt (type 7) signed by a host reset key that a management-signed key list (type 8) currently allows. |

### 3. Encoding and cryptography

All integers are big-endian and fixed width: u8, u16, u32, u64 unsigned, i32 two's complement. u64 values use exact integer arithmetic. `Fixed[n]` is exactly n bytes. `B(name)` is u8(byteLength) followed by the ASCII bytes. `||` is concatenation. The wire is binary. A signed structure has no optional fields, no padding, no trailing bytes. Implementations reject unsupported versions and types, invalid ranges and reserved bits, and consume every byte before any cryptography.

#### One suite

| Function | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| `H(x)` | BLAKE2b, 32-byte output, unkeyed. |
| `C(label)` | ASCII("SealedStream/5/" + label) \|\| 0x00. Labels are exact case-sensitive literals. |
| `D(seed, label)` | BLAKE2b with key = seed, message = `C(label)`, 32-byte output. |
| Signatures | Ed25519, detached, over `C(label) \|\| body`. Not Ed25519ph, no intermediate hash. |
| Records, vaults | XChaCha20-Poly1305 with a 32-byte key, a fresh random 24-byte nonce and the 16-byte tag appended. |
| Key wraps | libsodium `crypto_box_seal`: 32-byte `SK` becomes an 80-byte box. The nonce inside is BLAKE2b with 24-byte output over the ephemeral and recipient public keys, as libsodium defines it. |

The label prefix `SealedStream/5/` and the wire version byte 5 keep the protocol's earlier name on purpose: the prefix is hashed into every signature and every encryption's associated data, so Kavach v1 renames the SDK and the documents, not the wire, and every revision 2 vector still applies byte for byte. Names outside the wire carry Kavach's: the recovery code starts with `KV1R-` and a platform recovery entry with `kavach.recovery.` (revision 8; they were `SS5R-` and `ss5.recovery.` before).

#### Keys

| Key | Origin and use |
| --- | --- |
| `accountSeed` | 32 random bytes. `AK` is the Ed25519 keypair from this seed. Signs certificates and snapshots. Lives in the vault and the platform recovery item. Derives no encryption key. |
| `deviceSeed` | 32 random bytes per installation, never backed up. `DSK` = Ed25519 from `D(deviceSeed, "device-sign")`; `DEK` = `crypto_box_seed_keypair(D(deviceSeed, "device-box"))`. The seed is never used directly as an X25519 scalar. |
| `deviceId` | 16 random nonzero bytes per installation, new on every replacement. |
| `recoveryKey` | 32 random bytes. Encrypts the vault. Carried by the platform recovery provider and the recovery code. |
| `SK` | 32 random bytes per epoch. Never derived from any other key. |

#### Identifiers

- `identityId` = `H(C("identity") || B(accountName) || AK)`. Stable across device replacements; a succession (section 4) changes it. `accountId` is the `identityId` of the enrollment key and never changes.
- `streamId` = `H(C("stream") || identityId || deviceId)`. Changes with the device.
- `circleId` and `resetId` are independent random 16-byte values.
- `recordId` = `H(C("record-id") || streamId || u32(epoch) || u32(counter))`.
- `bodyHash` = `H(C("doc-hash") || body)`. The `certificateHash` is the type 1 body hash.

#### Documents

Types 1 to 4 and 7 to 9 have body = `u8(5) || u8(type) || fields` and wire = `body || signature[64]`. Type 6 is AEAD-protected and carries no signature. Type 5 was a signed join request in revision 2; it is retired, and a parser MUST reject it as `UNSUPPORTED_TYPE`.

| Type | Document | Signer and label | Wire length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | AccountDevice certificate | `AK`, `account-sig` | 187 + name length (188 to 251) |
| 2 | CircleSnapshot | Owner `AK`, `snapshot-sig` | 124 + 32 per member |
| 3 | EpochManifest | Author `DSK`, `epoch-sig` | 208 + 96 per audience row |
| 4 | EncryptedRecord | Author `DSK`, `record-sig` | 159 + plaintext length (159 to 4,255; the payload may be empty) |
| 5 | retired (was MembershipIntent) | none; rejected | none |
| 6 | AccountVault | none (AEAD under `recoveryKey`) | header 43 + name length, then 24 + 4 + ciphertext |
| 7 | AccountResetReceipt | Allowed reset key, `reset-sig` | 171 + name length |
| 8 | ResetKeyList | Management key, `reset-keys-sig` | 91 + 48 per key (0 to 8 keys) |
| 9 | AccountSuccession | Allowed reset key, `succession-sig` | 251 + name length |

#### Verification

Parse within bounds first, then verify the signature under the key the local trust state names, then apply continuity checks, then decrypt. Reject non-canonical Ed25519 signatures, invalid or small-order signing keys and X25519 keys that yield an all-zero shared secret. Only a type 1 certificate is verified under the `AK` it carries, and that establishes key possession only; every other verification key comes from an already pinned identity, or from the pinned management key for type 8. Two valid bodies at the same slot are `EQUIVOCATION`; an invalid signature is `BAD_SIGNATURE`.

### 4. Account and device

#### Type 1: AccountDevice certificate

```
body = u8(5) || u8(1) || B(accountName) || AK[32] || deviceGeneration:u64 || deviceId[16] || DSK.public[32] || DEK.public[32]
wire = body || Ed25519(AK, C("account-sig") || body)
```

Generation is nonzero. `deviceId` is nonzero and new for that account. `identityId` is constant across all of an account's certificates under one key; `streamId` changes with `deviceId`. The stream's author, as manifests and the host name it, is the account id. A certificate changes only when the device changes: there is no same-device re-certification, so an author's current manifest, which binds the author's certificate hash, stays valid until the author itself is replaced.

#### First contact and pinning

At first sight of an account, the SDK pins (`accountName`, `AK`, `accountId`) through the host's trust path. That path is the authenticated host directory, so enrollment is **host-trusted**: a malicious directory could substitute a key at first contact. The account id is the root of the chain the directory serves with the account, or the identity of the served key when the chain is empty. A served chain goes through the same path at first contact as for a pinned account (below, "Chains"): the policy gate first, then verification from the root to the served key; a client whose profile refuses successions pins nothing and quarantines the account, and a chain that does not verify or cannot yet be verified pins nothing either. Only a verified chain binds a served key to an account id. Fingerprint comparison between devices is supported for people who want to check, and does not change the wire. A later certificate MUST carry the same `accountName` and `AK`. A different `AK` for a pinned account is `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED`: the SDK quarantines the new key until a reset receipt (section 12) or, on a deployment that enables account recovery, a succession chain explains it, and never closes circles or re-admits on an unsigned directory change.

#### Generation rules

Per known account the SDK persists the greatest accepted `deviceGeneration` and its `certificateHash`. A lower generation is `DEVICE_ROLLBACK`. The same generation with the same hash is idempotent; with a different valid body it is `EQUIVOCATION`. A higher self-contained certificate under the same `AK` is accepted; gaps are legal. A reused `deviceId` is `DEVICE_ID_REUSED`, and the host also enforces uniqueness. The host's directory accepts a new certificate only by compare-and-swap on the exact current hash and generation plus one. An accepted higher certificate increments the reader's `desiredRevision`, emits `deviceChanged`, and rotates: wraps go only to the new `DEK`.

#### The two replacement models

| Model | How the new device proves it is the account | Who must be trusted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Identity continuity** | It holds the account key, recovered through the platform's synced secret store or the recovery code ("Replacing the device by identity continuity" below) | Nobody beyond first contact: the host cannot rebind a key |
| **Account recovery** | It holds nothing from the old device; the host's authority signs a succession after the product's fresh authentication ("Account recovery" below) | The host's signing authority, for every account's future traffic and, through owners, for every circle's membership (section 15) |

A profile declares which models it enables: `replacement: "continuity" | "recovery" | "both"`. The declaration is a property of the app build, decided by the product and never by a user: it is validated at `open` like the retention policy, has no runtime setter and changes only by shipping a new app version. **It is enforced by the peers who verify, not only by the device being replaced.** A client whose profile does not enable account recovery rejects every type 9 document as `UNSUPPORTED_TYPE`, whoever signed it, and keeps the account's key quarantined as `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED` exactly as for a bare directory key change; on such a deployment a host that tries to rebind a key obtains nothing from any client, which is what makes identity continuity host-independent in fact. A client whose profile does not enable identity continuity refuses `restoreAccount` as `NOT_AUTHORIZED`. A product that enables both has the convenience of both and the guarantee of account recovery, since every account on it can be rebound by the host; its profile states the enabled models and the resulting promise in one sentence.

#### Type 9: AccountSuccession

```
body = u8(5) || u8(9) || B(accountName) || accountId[32] || previousIdentityId[32] || newIdentityId[32] || previousGeneration:u64 || newGeneration:u64 || newCertificateHash[32] || successionId[16] || issuedAt:u64 || authorityKeyId[16]
wire = body || Ed25519(resetKey, C("succession-sig") || body)
```

Previous and new identity differ; `previousGeneration` is at least 1 and `newGeneration` is exactly one more; `successionId` is nonzero. The **transition** is (`accountId`, `previousIdentityId`, `previousGeneration`, `newIdentityId`, `newGeneration`, `newCertificateHash`, `successionId`); the signature and `issuedAt` are the **attestation**. The same transition under two attestations is one succession.

A peer that enables account recovery verifies a succession in this order, and the order is what keeps a key's removal from revoking what was verified under it. **One:** a transition already accepted stays trusted whatever happened to its key since; a second attestation of it is `DUPLICATE` and is not checked further. **Two:** an unseen attestation needs a fresh key list (section 12; `RESET_KEYS_REQUIRED` or `RESET_CLOCK_UNTRUSTED` defer it, sharing continues under the pinned key), an `authorityKeyId` the current list allows (`RESET_KEY_REVOKED` otherwise, whatever `issuedAt` says), and a valid signature under that key. **Three:** a validly attested transition that shares (`accountId`, `previousIdentityId`, `previousGeneration`) with an accepted one but differs from it is `EQUIVOCATION`: the authority signed two successors for one state, and the account is quarantined. **Four:** a new transition must name the account's pinned account id, a `previousIdentityId` equal to the current pinned identity and a `previousGeneration` at least the pinned generation (`ROLLBACK` below it; above it is legal, since identity-continuity activations the peer has not seen raise the generation under one key), a `newIdentityId` that is not a retired identity of the account (`SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`: `A → B → A` is refused), a `newCertificateHash` equal to the body hash of the certificate served with it, whose `accountName` matches and whose key derives `newIdentityId` and whose generation is `newGeneration` (`SUCCESSION_BINDING` otherwise), and a device id the account has not used (`DEVICE_ID_REUSED`). Acceptance commits in one transaction: the pin moves to the new key and certificate, the previous identity is retired with its key kept, the succession and a key-change notice are recorded, `desiredRevision` increments and the author rotates (T3), and `accountKeyChanged` and `deviceChanged` are emitted. Between successions the chain is contiguous: a succession's previous identity is the identity the prior one produced.

**Chains.** The directory serves an account whose key changed through successions with its chain from the account id, oldest first, in pages, each link with the certificate it binds, and with a certificate under the root key so that what the root key signed can be verified. Every succession, for a pinned contact, at first contact, for a device restoring under a succeeded key and for a device learning of its own retirement, follows one path with one set of transition rules (order four above, applied to an explicit state: the current identity and generation, the retired identities, the device ids used): the policy gate, the key list, then reconciliation, then each link verified in order against the state the previous one produced, page by page within the sync's read budget (section 13, `catchUp`), the verified prefix persisted after each page so that the next sync resumes at the next generation instead of starting over. Reconciliation drops the links a pinned contact already holds on record (the same transition at a recorded state), and they cost nothing against the budget; a different transition at a recorded state is `EQUIVOCATION` only once its attestation authenticates, since unauthenticated bytes are `BAD_SIGNATURE` and no evidence against anyone. A saved cursor is resumed however short the verified prefix is. The prefix's transitions were verified while their attestations were allowed, so they are replayed through the transition rules; every attestation is authenticated again before anything is recorded, and one whose signer has since left the list is fetched again from the host, which re-attests a legitimate transition under a current key (the same transition, or `EQUIVOCATION`). So an authority rotation never strands a partly downloaded chain, and a refused re-attestation leaves the pin where it was. A served chain is followed whenever there is one, even when the served key equals the pinned one: a certificate under a retired key at a higher generation is `SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`, never a plain device change. A first contact starts from the root with no generation and the identities the reset trust retired, so `A → B → A` is refused by a fresh device exactly as by an established peer. Only a complete chain moves anything: for a pinned contact every link is applied in one transaction, so the pin moves once and no epoch is ever sealed to an intermediate successor; for a first contact or a restore the verified links are recorded with the pin or the header. A chain the budget or the host did not finish is `METADATA_REQUIRED`, retryable; the account is excluded from every audience the peer seals from then until the chain completes, and sharing continues under the epoch already sealed to the pinned key. A link behind the pinned generation that is already on record is skipped. **Authority keys over time:** a transition verified while its key was allowed stays recognised after the key leaves the list; a new attestation under a removed key is refused; the host re-attests a link under a current key wherever its original signer is no longer allowed, and a re-attestation is the same succession.

#### Account recovery

On a fresh device with no secret from the old one, authenticated to the host as the account by the product's own login:

1. **Prepare** (`prepareReplacement()`, T11). The SDK resolves the account's current certificate, generation `g` and account id (the chain's root) through the directory, generates a fresh `accountSeed`, `deviceSeed`, `deviceId`, `recoveryKey` and random `successionId`, builds the certificate at generation `g + 1` under the new `AK` and a fresh vault at revision 1 with no entries, and persists the roots in the secret store and the exact bytes, the expected certificate hash and `g` in the store before returning the **proposal**: `{ successionId, accountName, accountId, expectedCertificateHash, expectedGeneration, certificate }`, all public. A second call returns the same pending proposal. The remote account is untouched; the device reports `replacementPrepared`.
2. **Authorize** (outside the SDK). The product performs its fresh authentication and asks the host to authorize the proposal. The host binds the authorization to the account, the succession id, the expected certificate hash and the proposal's certificate, single use, with an expiry, and returns an opaque token. A long-lived session alone MUST NOT obtain one. How the host authenticates is the product's; the protocol sees only the token.
3. **Commit** (`completeReplacement({ successionId, authorization })`). The SDK calls `succeedAccount`. The host, atomically: answers a repeated `successionId` with identical bytes `ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the original document **before** any authorization check, since a device retrying after a lost response finds its token spent; checks the authorization; compares and swaps the current certificate hash (`CONFLICT` with the current certificate otherwise); refuses a key equal to the current one, a wrong generation, a reused device id and a known id with different bytes as `UNAUTHORIZED`; signs the succession under its active operational key; stores the certificate and the vault; moves the current-device pointer; fences the previous device from every write and read; records the succession in the account's log; and returns the document. Opening the new device's stream is a completion step that must be idempotent and must also run when a retry finds the commit already made, and before the current device's first publication, since a device that finishes from the chain never repeats the commit call.
4. **Activate.** The SDK resolves the account's earlier chain, if any, and verifies it from the root to the previous identity through the same chain path a peer uses, bounded by the generation its own succession left from on every page alike, so a paged history never runs past the previous identity into the device's own link (a resolution the host does not answer is `METADATA_REQUIRED` with the proposal kept, and an empty history is accepted only when the previous identity is the account root); pins the previous certificate from the directory as any first contact would; verifies the returned succession against it exactly as a peer does; and only then, in one transaction, records the chain and every key it names, writes the T1 header under the new identity with the account id carried from the proposal, installs the vault, and enters `ready` (paused for rotation until its first epoch, like any activation); the recovery key goes to the provider. Holding the whole chain is what lets the device verify an owned circle's snapshot signed before an earlier replacement, and follow its own chain later to learn of its retirement. A succession that does not verify is `SUCCESSION_BINDING` (or `RESET_KEY_REVOKED`, `RESET_KEYS_REQUIRED`) and the device stays prepared with the proposal intact. Three outcomes of a commit that did not plainly succeed are kept apart: *unknown* (no answer): the SDK asks the chain for its own `successionId` and finishes from it, else reports `SUCCESSION_PENDING` and the same call is made again, never preparing another identity; *committed elsewhere*: `ALREADY_COMMITTED` finishes from the returned document, and so does the next `sync`, which asks the chain; *definitive conflict*: `CONFLICT`, the current certificate has moved and the chain does not hold our id, so the proposal is marked stale in the same transaction, its keys, never activated anywhere, are discarded, `REPLACEMENT_CONFLICT` names the current generation, and the next `prepareReplacement()` prepares against the current state. A refused authorization is `AUTHORIZATION_REQUIRED` and leaves the proposal intact. The SDK never retries a replacement on its own; the two calls are the whole API.
5. **Bootstrap** (section 5) runs inside ordinary `sync()` calls until done.
6. **Peers** verify the succession on their next sync and rotate. Until an author rotates, the new device sees that author as waiting, as today. The retired device, on learning the succession, enters `retired` and also erases its account seed, since that key is retired; it reports `SUPERSEDED_DEVICE` to any write. A device that cannot follow its own account's chain reports that in the sync report (`ownSuccession`): the host has fenced it already, and the report is what says why it still runs.

Two replacements racing for one account commit exactly one; the host's compare-and-swap orders them, and the other reports `REPLACEMENT_CONFLICT`.

#### Replacing the device by identity continuity

1. Recover the account: open the vault (section 5) with the recovery key from the platform provider or the saved recovery code. Verify the vault's account name and derive `AK` from the recovered seed before trusting anything in it. An account that succeeded cannot derive its account id from the current key: the device resolves the account through the directory, verifies the served chain link by link under a fresh key list from the root to the key it holds, takes the account id from the root and records the chain as evidence; the vault it opens is the one the succession installed, and it advances the generation under the current key.
2. Generate a fresh `deviceSeed`, `deviceId` and local store. Fetch the current certificate by the hash the vault pins and the snapshots of the vault's circles; verify them against the recovered pins.
3. Prepare the new certificate at generation plus one and a vault at its next revision. Persist both as exact bytes (T2), then call `activateDevice` with the expected current certificate hash and vault revision. A lost response is retried with identical bytes, never regenerated.
4. On commit the host moves the account's current-device pointer, fences the old device from all writes, and hints every circle the account belongs to. The new device starts epoch 1, counter 0 in its new stream once its audience is resolved and its first manifest is published.
5. Each author rotates to the new `DEK` on its next sync. Until an author rotates, every record it sends is sealed to the old certificate and never reaches the new device's subscription; the new device sees that the author's current manifest has no row for it, and the host's interface shows that person as waiting rather than nothing. Nothing waits on the circle owner.

Activation changes only the current-device pointer, the vault checkpoint and the transport fence. Nothing from the old device is copied: no outbox, no `SK`, no counters, no `deviceSeed`, no history. A missing or corrupt local store is handled by the same procedure under the preserved account, never by account reset. Every account reference, in both models, is resolved through the chain and never derived from the current key alone.

#### The retired device

A device that learns of a newer certificate for its own account enters `retired`: it stops all author and owner writes, cancels pending uploads, erases its device seed and working secrets best-effort (and its account seed when a succession retired the key), and keeps whatever local history the profile keeps. It MUST NOT delete the platform recovery item. Its `DEK` is the only key that opens the wraps addressed to it, so once every author has rotated it can read nothing new; that is the revocation a replacement provides, and it is complete only when the last author has synced. Replacement is not recovery from compromise of `accountSeed`: whoever holds that seed can authorize a device of their own, and only a reset ends that.

### 5. Account vault and recovery

#### Type 6: encrypted AccountVault

```
header = u8(5) || u8(6) || B(accountName) || AK[32] || vaultRevision:u64
AAD    = C("vault-aad") || header
wire   = header || nonce[24] || ciphertextLength:u32 || ciphertextAndTag

plaintext = accountSeed[32] || lastDeviceGeneration:u64 || lastCertificateHash[32] || circleCount:u16 || entries
entry     = circleId[16] || ownerIdentityId[32] || sharingEnabled:u8
```

Encrypt under `recoveryKey`. Revision is at least 1; ciphertext length is 90 to 4,990 bytes. Entries sort by `circleId` and are unique, count 0 to 100; `sharingEnabled` is 0 or 1. An entry pins a circle and the owner the user agreed to; whether the account is admitted or still waiting is read from the circle's current snapshot, never stored here. The vault holds no record payload, no `SK`, no `deviceSeed`, no `DEK`, no counters and no history: holding it with its recovery key authorises a device, and with it future access, but decrypts no history. After decryption, verify the account name and that `AK` derives from the recovered seed before trusting the entries.

#### Recovery order

1. **Platform recovery provider.** The platform's synced secret store hands back the `recoveryKey` for this account on a new device signed into the same platform account. No code, no old device.
2. **Saved recovery code.** `"KV1R-" || base64url(recoveryKey)`, 48 characters, available on demand after local authentication. It opens the latest vault the server returns and has no freshness anchor.
3. **Explicit destructive reset** (section 12), only when the user confirms, never automatically.

Every path yields the same account key, circles and preferences, and fresh device keys. When both recovery paths fail the SDK returns `ACCOUNT_RECOVERY_REQUIRED` and preserves the circle state it cannot use. It never manufactures replacement identity keys. Enrollment never blocks on the recovery code: the SDK saves the key through the provider and returns; the code is offered later as the way to complete recovery when the provider cannot.

#### Recovery provider contract

The SDK takes a `RecoveryProvider` with `load(accountName, identityId)`, `save(accountName, identityId, recoveryKey)`, `status()` and `eraseLocalAccess()`. Results carry a status (`available`, `unavailable`, `locked`, `unsupported`, `error`), a stable reason and a `backupState` (`unknown`, `pending`, `unavailable`). A successful local write is never reported as a completed cloud backup. The host authenticates the account before the provider releases a secret; the SDK verifies the recovered vault before activation. Logout or retirement erases local access without deleting the shared item. Provider failure never strands enrollment and never triggers a reset. The first sync of every process asks the provider for this identity's key and saves it again when the provider has nothing, so a process that dies between activation and the save cannot leave the account unbacked.

#### Bootstrap without a vault

A device that arrives by account recovery holds no vault, so it learns its circles from the host. **Discovery is host-trusted at the level this section already grants enrollment**: the host authenticates the account and serves `listCircles(cursor)`, for each circle the account owns, belongs to or has a pending request in, the `circleId`, the owner's account id, the account's role, the current snapshot and the account's sharing preference with its revision. Pages are bounded; the client persists each page's results and the cursor before fetching the next, and a restart resumes at the cursor without erasing a circle already pinned. For each circle the client resolves the owner's chain, verifies the snapshot under a key in the owner's verified chain (the current one, or a retired one, since the circle is at revision 0 here), and pins (`circleId`, owner account id) only when the snapshot names this account as the host says; a pending request is host state and is pinned as `requested`. Discovery is not permission to share: the circle is enabled only if the served preference says so, and an absent preference is disabled. A closed circle is not reopened. A circle that cannot be adopted on this sync (its owner unresolved, its snapshot refused) is kept with what the host served and retried by every sync, named by its circle id in the report's `bootstrap` error; bootstrap is complete only when every page is read and nothing waits, and a failed circle never undoes another.

**Currency is host-trusted, and that is a limit, not a guarantee.** A fresh device has no revision checkpoint, so it cannot tell the current snapshot from an older, correctly signed one, nor a preference the user set from one the host asserts. A malicious host can roll a fresh device back to a snapshot from before a removal, a departure or a closure, or serve `enabled: true` for a circle the user disabled; what bounds it is the same as for a rolled-back vault: the device can only share with members an owner once admitted, under keys it verifies. Receiving an acceptable newer snapshot corrects it; a conflicting snapshot at a revision the device already holds quarantines the circle. The client writes no membership during bootstrap. An owner's successor republishes the snapshot it was served at the next revision in its first bootstrap sync, so a host-served rollback reaches the owner's next revision only if the host also served it to the owner.

The **sharing preference** lives at the host as well as in the vault: a flag per (account, circle) with a revision the host keeps, written by the client at the join request, at circle creation and on every enable or disable (`setSharingPreference(circleId, enabled, expectedRevision)`, compare-and-swap), and returned by discovery. Idempotence alone would let a delayed enable overwrite a later disable with an honest host; the revision orders them. The client persists the pending change with the revision it expects and applies it locally in the same transaction, so a disable stops sharing at once whether or not the host has heard of it, and sync retries it until it lands or conflicts; on `CONFLICT` the device's **latest persisted user action** wins, read from the store at retry time and never taken from the request that conflicted, and is written again at the served revision. Each pending change carries a local sequence, a response clears only the sequence it answered, and a late success for an older request never moves the revision back. Bootstrap marks the vault dirty, so the fresh vault the succession installed carries the discovered entries on the next publication and identity continuity works on the next replacement.

#### Vault updates

Vault changes are control-plane work and rare: a join request adds an entry before the request is sent, a leave removes it, enabling or disabling sharing updates the preference, and device activation records the new generation and certificate hash. Observed snapshots never touch the vault, and owner edits publish the snapshot alone. The SDK persists the exact encrypted bytes, increments the revision and publishes by compare-and-swap on the previous revision and hash; on conflict it fetches and decrypts the current vault and recomputes. A recovering device fetches each pinned circle's current snapshot, verifies it under the pinned owner identity, and learns from it whether the account is admitted or still waiting. A server can withhold or replay an old vault; a fresh device cannot detect that, and what it loses is a circle pin or a preference, never a key or a membership the owner granted. Section 15 states that limit.

### 6. Circle membership and owner approval

#### Type 2: CircleSnapshot

```
body = u8(5) || u8(2) || circleId[16] || ownerIdentityId[32] || revision:u64 || memberCount:u16 || memberIdentityId[32] x memberCount
wire = body || Ed25519(ownerAK, C("snapshot-sig") || body)
```

Members are account ids (section 2): they sort strictly, are unique, count 1 to 100, and include the owner exactly once. A succession changes no snapshot: the account keeps its id, and the owner's successor signs the next revision under the new key. Resolving members MUST reject two different account keys for one account name. Creation is revision 1 with the owner as sole member; the SDK pins (`circleId`, `ownerIdentityId`) at creation or at a user-confirmed join, never from a server-supplied list. Per circle the SDK persists the highest accepted revision and body hash. A higher self-contained snapshot may skip intermediates; a lower revision is `ROLLBACK`; the same revision with the same body is `DUPLICATE`, with a different valid body `EQUIVOCATION`, which quarantines the circle. A device change neither resets the revision nor creates a snapshot. A restored owner fetches the current snapshot and signs revision plus one; the host's compare-and-swap prevents overwriting a newer one.

#### Join and leave requests

A request to join or leave is host state, not a document. The host already authenticates the account that makes it, and the owner's trust in that account's key comes from the directory pin, exactly as for every other account. A signed request would prove only what the host's authentication already establishes, at the cost of a document type, an expiry policy and a replay list, so revision 3 removed it. Requests are transient and never enter a membership chain: only the owner's next snapshot changes membership.

| Action | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Request to join | The requester stores the pinned circle (`circleId`, `ownerIdentityId`) and its sharing preference in its vault, then calls `requestJoin` on the host, authenticated as its account. The host records the pending request and notifies the owner visibly, naming the requester and nothing else. A link or short code the host hands the requester is a routing hint, never membership authority. |
| Owner approves | The owner reads the pending request, resolves and pins the requester's (`accountName`, `AK`) through the directory, builds the successor snapshot from trusted local state plus this one change, commits it (T8) and publishes it by compare-and-swap. The host clears the request when the snapshot that admits the account is published. Approval of an admitted account is idempotent. A successor the host has not yet confirmed stays pending locally (T8) and is published again by every sync; when it lands, the commit settles membership only and keeps the sharing preference the user set meanwhile. |
| Start sharing | `requestJoin` defaults to sharing enabled on approval, because that is the user's explicit request; the host may pass false. |
| Replace a device | The snapshot does not change. Verify the member's new certificate under the pinned account key, or its succession chain where the profile enables account recovery, and rotate to its new device key. A snapshot signed by an owner's retired key is accepted only for a circle the device holds at revision 0 (bootstrap, first contact), and the bytes a device already verified stay a duplicate whatever key signed them; an established circle advances under the current owner key alone, so a retired key can never change membership. A snapshot under a key no verified chain produced is `BAD_SIGNATURE`. |
| Leave | `leave` disables local sending, removes the vault entry and stops opening that circle's authors immediately, then files a leave request so the owner removes the membership. |
| Remove | The owner's explicit action: publish the next snapshot without that identity. Each author pauses and rotates when it learns the change. |
| Close | Only explicit `closeCircle` closes a circle: it prepares an owner-only snapshot (T8), publishes it, forgets the members' keys and removes the vault entry; a circle with no other member closes locally with the same vault removal. A close whose publication failed is finished by the next sync, vault entry included. Device loss, replacement or absence never closes a circle. The owner cannot leave without closing. |

A pending request expires on the host after a profile-chosen time; a rejected or expired request cannot block unrelated changes. A fabricated request shown by a malicious host yields nothing a first-contact key substitution could not: the owner admits the account the request names, under the key the directory serves, and section 15 counts both under host-trusted enrollment.

### 7. Audience and reconciliation

An **eligible circle** is one the account has locally joined and enabled, whose snapshot contains this account, whose owner pin is verified, and which is neither closed nor quarantined. The SDK keeps using the last verified snapshot while reporting its age; a malicious server may withhold a newer removal, and section 15 says so.

The **desired audience** is the author's own account id plus every member of every eligible circle, deduplicated. Each account id resolves to its greatest verified current certificate; an audience entry is (`accountId`, `certificateHash`, `DEK.public`), and the manifest's identity field carries the account id. Exactly one current device per account receives a wrap. An ambiguous name-to-key mapping is rejected; a changed `AK` is a recovery or security error, never silent substitution; an account retired by a reset receipt, and an account whose succession chain this device has not finished verifying, are out of the audience. A self-only audience is valid.

**Desired revision.** The author persists `desiredRevision` and increments it for a newly observed relevant snapshot revision, an accepted recipient device change, its own device activation, a join, leave, enable or disable, counter exhaustion or hygiene rotation. It increments even when a newer snapshot's final member set is unchanged, so a missed remove-then-rejoin still rotates. The same transaction (T3) invalidates the active epoch, burns allocated counters, cancels not-yet-handed-off ciphertext sealed under the obsolete revision, and sets `paused(rotation)` before any callback runs. Bytes already handed to the network cannot be recalled, which is why section 11 serialises the handoff with this transaction.

If any required identity, snapshot or current-device proof is missing, return `METADATA_REQUIRED`, pause enqueue and publication, and never guess, omit recipients or reuse a known-obsolete key. A valid conflicting signature quarantines the affected state; invalid external bytes alone never replace valid cached state.

**Sync cadence.** The SDK synchronises on connection, resume, host metadata hints and at a profile-defined interval while online; the host forwards background wake-ups. After a reconnect or any transport uncertainty, synchronise before draining the outbox. On accepting a new recipient certificate, emit `deviceChanged` and rotate immediately; after rotation emit `refresh` so the host supplies a fresh record of each kind with a slot rather than resending an old one.

### 8. Epoch manifest and addressed wraps

#### Type 3: EpochManifest

```
body = u8(5) || u8(3) || streamId[32] || authorIdentityId[32] || authorCertificateHash[32] || epoch:u32 || createdAt:u64
       || keyCommitment[32] || audienceCount:u16 || rows
row  = recipientIdentityId[32] || recipientCertificateHash[32] || wrapHash[32]
wire = body || Ed25519(DSK, C("epoch-sig") || body)

keyCommitment = H(C("epoch-key") || streamId || u32(epoch) || SK)
wrap          = crypto_box_seal(SK, recipient DEK.public)        -- exactly 80 bytes
wrapHash      = H(C("wrap") || wrap)
```

Rows sort strictly by identity id, are unique, count 1 to 100, and include the author exactly once. A reader fetches the author's certificate by its hash, verifies it under the pinned `AK`, and requires that its derived identity id and stream id match the manifest and that it is the author's current certificate before using the epoch for a newest slot. The reader's own row must name its current certificate hash. Before storing `SK`, verify the manifest signature, the certificate bindings, the own row, the wrap hash, sealed-box authentication and the key commitment. Signing is required because every member knows `SK`.

#### Publication

| Step | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Prepare | Resolve the full audience; choose `highestCommittedEpoch + 1`; generate `SK`, one wrap per row; sign once. Persist the exact manifest, all wraps, `SK` and the captured `desiredRevision` in one local transaction (T4) before any network call. At most one unresolved epoch per stream: a context that finds one adopts it and never overwrites it, since a second preparation carries a different key. |
| Publish | `publishEpoch` compares the host's current epoch and hash, installs the bundle atomically and raises the stream's epoch fence. A timeout is retried with identical bytes; a lost response is resolved by lookup: matching bytes mean committed, a different valid body at that epoch is `EQUIVOCATION`. Never regenerate a signature, nonce or key for a prepared slot. |
| Promote | After commit, persist `highestCommittedEpoch`. Promote to active only if the captured `desiredRevision` and the exact certificate-bound audience still match current state; promotion stores the active `SK` and `nextCounter = 0` in one transaction (T5). Promotion is idempotent: an epoch already active is left as it is, counter included. A promoted epoch clears the rotation, metadata and limit pauses it resolves. |
| Obsolete | Otherwise the epoch is committed but obsolete: never seal with it, prepare the next one. |
| Exhausted | At counter 2^32 - 1, on clock reversal, or when the epoch is older than the hygiene interval, the next enqueue returns `ROTATION_REQUIRED` and a new epoch is prepared. |

#### Retention of epoch material

An epoch's manifest, wraps and author certificate stay on the host while an undelivered record references the epoch or the epoch is the author's current one; when neither holds the host deletes them. A reader keeps an opened `SK` until every record of that epoch it holds has been decrypted into local history or has expired. Generation and epoch checkpoints are persisted separately from key deletion. A replacement device never fetches old-device wraps or keys. An unexplained higher committed epoch for the local stream means local state was lost; the device replaces its stream under the same account rather than guessing counters. A self-wrap recovers `SK`, never a counter.

### 9. Records and kinds

#### Type 4: EncryptedRecord

```
header    = u8(5) || u8(4) || streamId[32] || epoch:u32 || counter:u32 || sentAt:u64 || flags:u8     -- 51 bytes
flags     : bit 0 reserved, MUST be zero; bits 1-2 = header class (0 to 3); bits 3-7 zero
plaintext = u8(1) || kind:u8 || eventTime:u64 || payload                                           -- the envelope, 10 bytes, then 0 to 4,096
AAD       = C("record-aad") || header
body      = header || nonce[24] || ciphertextLength:u32 || ciphertextAndTag
wire      = body || Ed25519(DSK, C("record-sig") || body)
```

The record's kind travels inside the encryption, in the envelope: a version byte, the kind, and the event time the sender gave (0 when none: a sender that passes zero sends none, and orders its own slot the same way). The header shows only the kind's declared header class, the one thing the relay may learn about a record's kind, for routing and notifications without content. Bit 0 carried an urgency flag in an earlier revision and stays reserved; before revision 8 the two class bits carried the kind itself (0 with neither bit, 1 with bit 1, 2 with bit 2, both set invalid) and the payload was the whole plaintext. One global counter spans every kind in an epoch. `sentAt` is the public send time in Unix milliseconds, not an authoritative capture time; `eventTime` is the sender's claim about what the record describes and a reader never trusts it past `sentAt`. Encrypt with the active `SK` and a fresh nonce; ciphertext length is plaintext length plus 16, between 26 and 4,122. The signature covers the entire body; the AAD covers the header. A retry resends the persisted bytes and never changes nonce, counter or `sentAt`.

**Verification.** Resolve the author's current certificate and the manifest for (`streamId`, `epoch`); verify stream and certificate bindings, then the `DSK` signature, then decrypt, then read the envelope. Zero epoch, reserved flags, out-of-range lengths, trailing bytes and an envelope version other than 1 are invalid. A known superseded author device cannot take a newest slot (`SUPERSEDED_DEVICE`); its previously accepted records may still be verified under the archived certificate and manifest and stored as history.

#### Kinds are declared by the product

The protocol carries a kind as a small integer and gives it no meaning. The product declares, in the retention policy it passes to `open()`, every kind it uses and how the SDK treats it: `retentionMs`, how long a reader keeps records of the kind and the payload of its newest slot, from `sentAt`; `queue`, `keepAll` (every queued record is sent) or `latestWins` (a newer queued record of the kind cancels the older one with reason `superseded`, never one already handed to the network); `history`, whether records are kept in indexed, pageable history; `newest`, whether one newest-per-author slot is kept (section 10); and `headerClass`, the two header bits the relay may see for the kind (0 to 3, 0 when absent). A kind must keep at least one of history and a slot. A record of a kind the reader did not declare is `invalid(BAD_PAYLOAD)` and is not stored; an enqueue of one is `MALFORMED`. The envelope carries kinds 0 to 255. A product's declarations, with its payload codecs, are in its profile; the first profile declares a position (history and a slot), an event (history, no slot, every one news) and a state (a slot only, latest wins while queued).

#### Payloads

A payload is 0 to 4,096 opaque bytes the product defines and versions in its profile. The SDK never parses one: not to order it (the envelope's event time does that), not to notify about it (the header class does that), not to decide whether to keep it (the kind's declaration does that). A product that needs a kind to carry no names, addresses or device identifiers says so in its codec, where the protocol cannot.


### 10. Delivery and deletion

#### Publishing

`enqueue(kind, payload, options)` runs inside one write transaction (T6): answer an idempotency key the caller gave before, otherwise require a usable active epoch whose `desiredRevision` equals current state, enforce the profile's queue limits, encrypt and sign once, insert the complete wire into the outbox, write the author's own history entry and its index entries (a slot-only kind writes none) and the idempotency key when given, and increment `nextCounter`. In the same transaction the sender's own row follows the reader's rules: a record of a kind with a slot is offered to the sender's own slot by the order in Receiving below, and one of a kind with history is written there, so `newest(identityId, kind)` answers for the sender's own identity as for any author and `recordStored` fires for self. `queued(recordId)` is returned only after commit. A queued record of a `keepAll` kind is never coalesced or replaced; a queued record of a `latestWins` kind yields to a newer one of the same kind (section 9). `QUEUE_FULL` is explicit.

A single serialised sender drains the outbox in (epoch, counter) order with one record in flight per stream: the next handoff waits until the previous outcome is durable, so accepted (epoch, counter) pairs reach the relay in strictly increasing order and the relay's append checkpoint below stays small. For each record it opens a write transaction, re-checks device status, epoch, `desiredRevision` and expiry, marks the row in flight, **initiates the handoff to the transport inside that transaction**, and commits; only awaiting the response happens outside (T6h). Because T3 needs the same lock, no authorization change can commit between the check and the handoff. A timeout retries the same bytes while still eligible. A known change cancels pending uploads sealed for the obsolete audience and emits `cancelled(recordId, reason)`. Old keys are never re-wrapped to newly admitted accounts to deliver a backlog.

#### Relay contract: deliver, then delete

**The server keeps a record only until every recipient has it.** The relay is a per-author stream with one cumulative cursor per reader, not an archive and not a per-record ledger. It is trusted for delivery and expiry bookkeeping, never for confidentiality or authenticity: it can lose or expire a record, which is an availability failure, and it can never forge one.

1. **Append.** `appendRecord(recordId, wire)` verifies the caller's authorization and the current device and epoch fences, stores the immutable ciphertext once, and assigns it the stream's next `deliverySeq` (a u64, strictly increasing, never reused) and an acceptance time that never decreases, in one transaction. It is idempotent by `recordId` and exact bytes: a retry returns the original sequence; a different body for a used id is `EQUIVOCATION`; a retry after deletion is answered by the append checkpoint below and is never reinserted. A new record can only be appended for an epoch whose manifest the relay already holds and that is the author's highest published one; after verifying the author device, the relay consults the append checkpoint below before applying that epoch fence, so a retry of the previous epoch's last record gets its original outcome rather than a rejection, and returning that outcome appends nothing.
2. **Readers.** A reader's subscription to a stream is bound to (`streamId`, `recipientIdentityId`, `recipientCertificateHash`). The host creates it at the stream's current tail when the reader is admitted, when the reader activates a device, and for every member when an author opens a new stream, and it holds one `ackCursor` starting there. A replacement device gets a new subscription at its activation; it never inherits the old device's cursor, and records sealed to the old certificate are never re-addressed to it.
3. **Read.** `readRecords(streamId, afterSeq, limit)` answers from one consistent view of the stream and the caller's subscription. It scans strictly above `scanStart = max(afterSeq, committedCursor, expiredThrough)` and rejects an `afterSeq` beyond the tail. It returns, in sequence order, every retained record in `(scanStart, nextSeq]` whose epoch addresses this subscription's certificate, together with `nextSeq`, the stream's `tail`, its `expiredThrough` watermark and the subscription's committed cursor. `nextSeq` is the last sequence fully examined, never an assumed tail: when the limit is reached the page stops before the first eligible record it could not include, and an empty page may still advance through an examined interval that held nothing eligible. It writes nothing; the reader's scan position is local state. A sequence inside the interval that is not returned was not addressed to this subscription, was deleted after every required reader acknowledged it, or lies within the expired prefix; the reader acknowledges through `nextSeq` without telling these apart, and only once every returned record is durably processed and every earlier blocker it knows of is resolved or expired. A page limit or a later page never clears an earlier blocker. The relay is trusted for delivery: withholding a record is an availability failure it could commit through expiry in any case, and it is never a forgery.
4. **Acknowledge.** `ackThrough(streamId, cursor)` rejects a cursor above the tail with `CURSOR_OUT_OF_RANGE` and otherwise sets the subscription's cursor to max(stored, requested), authenticated to the exact reader device and subscription. An equal or older cursor is a successful no-op with no write. Success is reported only after durable commit. There is no per-record acknowledgement and no exception list.
5. **Completion and immediate deletion.** A record's required readers are the rows of its epoch's manifest other than the author. When a cursor advances, the relay deletes every record whose sequence is now at or below the cursor of every required reader, in the same transaction as the cursor change. The minimum is taken over the epoch's audience, never over every subscriber the stream ever had. A self-only epoch's records complete on append. No copy survives on the server: record bodies are excluded from the host's logs, analytics, backups and dead-letter queues, which the deletion promise covers. Physical erasure of replicas is operational policy the client cannot prove.
6. **Expiry.** One delivery window applies to every record of a stream. A record expires at its acceptance time plus the window; because acceptance times never decrease, expiry advances as a prefix. The relay keeps one monotonic `expiredThrough` watermark per stream, deletes the ciphertext of the expired prefix, and returns the watermark on every read so a reader can cross the prefix without asking for records that no longer exist. It never expires a newer record because an older one expired. The author's own history is then the only copy on the author's side; readers that already stored a record keep it. Expiry is an availability fact, never a receipt.
7. **Append checkpoint.** The relay keeps per stream one high-water (epoch, counter) pair and the outcome of the last append (`recordId`, body hash, `deliverySeq`, acceptance time), updated atomically with each append. Within the profile's `appendRetryWindowMs`, a retry matching the last outcome returns it even after the ciphertext was deleted; a different body at that slot is `EQUIVOCATION`. An append at or below the high-water pair with no matching evidence is `STALE_APPEND`: nothing is reinserted and no success is invented. The client records that record as `acceptanceUnknown`, keeps its own history entry, stops retrying it and, for a kind with a slot, emits `refresh`; it never re-creates a record on its own, which could duplicate an event. Appends for a retired device stream are refused by the certificate fence and never recreate deleted stream state.
8. **Epoch material.** A manifest, its wraps and the author's certificate are retained while the epoch is current or any retained record references it, and deleted when neither holds; a certificate shared by several epochs or by trust state stays while any of them still needs it. The expiry watermark is one less than the sequence of the oldest retained record whose deadline has not passed, or the tail when nothing is retained, so records already deleted by acknowledgement need no timestamps of their own.
9. Sequences, cursors and watermarks are transport state, never authorization. A missing record is `NOT_FOUND`, not proof that it never existed.
10. The host decides notifications from the header class alone, by the product's declaration (the first profile maps class 1 to a visible notification to each recipient, class 0 to a silent wake-up, class 2 to nothing). The host never learns the kind, and no notification carries record content.

#### Receiving

Only accept authors currently admitted in a locally joined circle, or self. `receive(wire)` resolves the author's certificate and the manifest for (`streamId`, `epoch`), fetching the manifest and the reader's wrap through `getEpoch` when they are not local, verifies the complete record, and in one transaction (T7) inserts it by `recordId` into local history with its index entries when its kind keeps history, records a disposition, and, when its kind keeps a newest slot, takes the author's slot for that kind only if the record is from the author's current device and it orders after the slot's holder: a newer device generation first; within one generation the later event time, which is the envelope's `eventTime` clamped to the record's `sentAt`, so a device clock set ahead cannot hold the slot against later honest records; equal event times by (epoch, counter). A record with no event time orders at its `sentAt`. The SDK never reads a payload to order it. Replays are harmless upserts; a different valid body for a retained `recordId` is `EQUIVOCATION`. There is no application-level acknowledgement: relay acknowledgement is internal to the SDK.

**Acknowledgement rule.** Per author stream the SDK keeps a local acknowledged prefix and advances it only across a contiguous run of sequences in which every record addressed to this device has been durably stored, or that the relay has declared expired. It sends at most one `ackThrough` per stream per processed batch, after T7 commits. A record it cannot open yet because it is offline and cannot fetch the epoch is `keyPending`: it is not stored, the prefix holds at it, and records beyond it are still read, verified and stored, so the next acknowledgement covers them. The relay returns only records addressed to this subscription; a returned record whose verified manifest nevertheless has no row for this device is `invalid(NOT_AUTHORIZED)` and does not hold the prefix, because this device is not among its required readers. Acknowledging a `keyPending` record early would let the relay delete the only copy of the key material, and a later rotation cannot recover an earlier epoch's random key. Crossing an expired prefix is queue progress, never a receipt.

Records arrive out of counter order and that is normal: counter 6 after counter 7 is inserted into the trail and cannot move a newest slot backward. Nor can a record with an earlier event time sent later under a higher counter, as when one runtime flushes a buffered batch after another sent a fresh record: it joins the trail and the slot keeps the later event. The SDK never waits for contiguous counters. Records from a superseded device populate history when the receiver holds the original key; they cannot overwrite the current device's slot. Removing a member clears that member's slots and keys; it does not erase history already received. No peer key requests exist: the only sources of a key are the author's manifest and the author's next rotation.

| `receive` result | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `updated` | New record stored and it is news: it took its kind's newest slot, or its kind keeps no slot and every record counts. |
| `history` | New record stored in the trail because the slot already holds a newer one, or the kind keeps no slot and it is not news. |
| `older` | Verified, of a slot-only kind, and older than the slot's holder or from a retired device: nothing kept. |
| `duplicate` | Same bytes already stored. Acknowledge, do not reinsert. |
| `keyPending` | Not stored; the acknowledged prefix holds; read again after the next sync. A manifest or wrap that does not verify leaves the record here with its code named, never refused: the record may be honest, and the relay's expiry resolves one that never verifies. |
| `expired` | Inside the relay's expired prefix, or past `sentAt + 5 minutes` of future skew (`CLOCK_SKEW`). |
| `invalid(reason)` | `BAD_SIGNATURE`, `DECRYPT_FAILED`, `SUPERSEDED_DEVICE`, `NOT_AUTHORIZED`, `EQUIVOCATION` or a parse error. Nothing is updated; the code is named to the app as `securityError`. |

`newest(identityId, kind)` is a view over the kind's slot: the verified record, the event time it was chosen by and its age; a stale slot is never presented as live. A replacement device subscribes at its activation boundary and receives only records appended after it; its view fills as authors rotate, person by person.

### 11. Durable state

This section states what the local store must guarantee. Which database or secret store provides it is a profile decision.

#### Requirements

- **Secrets.** `accountSeed`, `deviceSeed`, `recoveryKey` and the store's own key live in the platform's protected secret store, device-only. Only the `recoveryKey` is ever placed in a synced platform item. The local store, device seed, counters and outbox are excluded from automatic device backup.
- **Encryption at rest.** Every row of the local store, including plaintext history, is encrypted at rest under a per-installation key held in the secret store. No plaintext secret appears in a row key, an index or a log.
- **Binding.** A `meta` row binds schema version, a random store instance id, `identityId`, `accountId` and the current `certificateHash`, and records whether the history index and the newest slots below are complete, and where bootstrap after account recovery stands. A mismatch between the secret store and the local store pauses with `RECOVERY_REQUIRED` and is resolved by device replacement, never by inventing counters.
- **One writer.** All writes from every execution context of the app run under one mutual-exclusion primitive that spans processes. Reads that only inspect state may run outside it.
- **The handoff.** The lock protects only what happens inside it, so the one network operation whose ordering matters, the handoff of an outbound record, is initiated inside it (T6h). Every other network call runs outside, and its result is recorded in a new transaction that first revalidates `desiredRevision`, the certificate generation and the epoch; a context whose revalidation fails discards its result.
- **Bounded waiting.** A writer that cannot acquire the lock fails with `LOCKED`, a retryable error, without allocating counters or keys.
- **Bounded reads.** The store serves an ordered key range under a limit without reading the rest of a table. History carries a time-ordered index, two keys per row (the timeline of a kind, and one author's), written with the row in T6 and T7 and removed with it by the retention purge; queries, cursors and the purge read a slice of that index, never the history table. A store written before the index exists is indexed once on open and the header records it. The newest slots are one table keyed by author and kind, holding the order fields and the payload; a store from before it rebuilds the slots from history on open.

#### Transaction boundaries

| Tx | What commits together |
| --- | --- |
| T1 | Initialisation: roots in the secret store, then the store header with an `INITIALIZING` marker, then `ACTIVE` only after the pending certificate is durable. |
| T2 | Exact device certificate and vault bytes prepared for activation. |
| T3 | Verified circle or device change, `desiredRevision` increment, active `SK` invalidation, cancellation of obsolete pending records. |
| T4 | Exact manifest, wraps, `SK` and audience checkpoint for a prepared epoch. |
| T5 | Epoch publication outcome and conditional promotion with `nextCounter = 0`. |
| T6 | Outbound record into the outbox and local history with its index entries, the idempotency key when given, counter increment. |
| T6h | Handoff: eligibility re-check, in-flight mark and the send call for one outbound record. |
| T7 | Inbound record with its index entries, disposition, conditional newest slot, and the acknowledgement cursor under the rule in section 10. |
| T8 | Owner snapshot: the successor revision and its hash, built from local state plus one change; kept pending until the host confirms it, and published again by every sync until then. |
| T9 | A standalone vault update before publication, kept pending and published again by every sync until confirmed; or an accepted reset-key list; or an accepted reset receipt together with every effect it has: the closures, the rotation and the forgotten author. |
| T10 | Own reset: the new account, device and recovery roots in the secret store and the exact new certificate, vault and `resetId` in the store, before the reset is sent. |
| T11 | Own replacement proposal (account recovery): the fresh roots in the secret store and the exact certificate, vault, `successionId`, expected certificate hash and generation in the store, before anything is sent; kept until the proposal commits or is definitively conflicted. Activation after the commit writes the verified succession, the header under the new identity and the vault in one transaction. |

#### Required crash outcomes

Before T4 commits, no epoch is publishable; after T4 but before the network succeeds, restart publishes exactly the saved bytes; after remote commit but before T5, lookup finishes T5 without new randomness. Before T6 commits, no `recordId` is returned and nothing is sent; after T6, restart retries the exact wire or cancels it with a disposition, never decrementing the counter; a row marked in flight by T6h whose response was lost is resolved by lookup, never resent under a changed audience. After T7, restart reads history, the slots and the cursor even if callbacks were lost. Before activation commits, the new device stays `activationPending` and emits nothing; after remote activation but before local acknowledgement, lookup finishes that activation and never allocates another device; a pending activation whose prepared bytes the host refuses because the vault moved, while it still shows the expected certificate, is prepared again with the same device keys. Before T10 commits, no reset is sent; after T10, restart or the next sync sends the same `resetId` and finishes under the keys the host holds, never generating others. A receipt is never on record without its effects. Before T11 commits, no proposal exists; after T11, restart returns the same proposal; after the host committed but before the device has the succession, the next `completeReplacement` or `sync` finishes from the chain or from `ALREADY_COMMITTED`; a verified committed identity is never replaced by its predecessor. A succession accepted for a contact is never on record without its key-change notice, and a restart after the acceptance sees the same chain as duplicates. Every retry sends exact persisted bytes, so a lost response, a refused call and a crash all converge on the next sync. These outcomes are tested against the real store, not only an in-memory model.

### 12. Last-resort account reset and the reset keys

Normal device replacement, by either model of section 4, keeps membership and ownership. `resetAccount` is a separate destructive action taken only after every replacement path has failed, after fresh host re-authentication and an explicit explanation: new account and recovery keys, new device keys, peers see `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED`, membership continuity is lost, re-admission is required, and circles owned by the old account close. The immutable `accountName` stays. A failed background key lookup never invokes reset. **After successions:** a reset retires the whole chain and the account id, not only the current key: the receipt names the current identity as old, a receipt naming a retired identity is `RESET_BINDING`, peers close every circle the account id owns and stop sharing to it, no later succession may name any identity of the retired chain, and the new identity is a new account root with an empty chain. A reset and a succession for one account cannot both commit against one current state: the host's compare-and-swap orders them, and a peer that sees both for one previous state treats the pair as `EQUIVOCATION`.

The old `AK` may be gone, so it cannot sign its own replacement or its circles' closure. A **host reset authority** signs the receipt instead, with operational keys that a management key can retire; the same keys sign successions (section 4) on a deployment that enables account recovery. The SDK pins one deployment-specific Ed25519 management public key and a minimum list version; the management private key stays offline, separate from the online signer. On a continuity-only deployment the authority can retire identities and close circles and never admit a member or decrypt a record; on a deployment that enables account recovery it can also rebind an account's key, with the consequences section 15 states. An attacker holding an allowed operational key can deny service until that key is removed from the list.

#### Type 8: ResetKeyList

```
body = u8(5) || u8(8) || version:u64 || issuedAt:u64 || expiresAt:u64 || keyCount:u8 || rows
row  = authorityKeyId[16] || Ed25519PublicKey[32]
wire = body || Ed25519(managementKey, C("reset-keys-sig") || body)
```

Rows sort strictly by key id; ids and keys are unique, ids nonzero, keys valid points, count 0 to 8. Version starts at 1. `issuedAt < expiresAt` and the lifetime is at most 30 days; a profile renews well inside that. An empty list disables every new reset. Operational keys MUST differ from the management key and cannot sign lists. A retired id is never reused and a compromised key is never re-authorised.

`getResetKeyList()` returns opaque bytes. Verify under the pinned management key; require version at least the packaged minimum and at least the durable highest version. Equal version with equal body hash is a duplicate; equal version with a different valid body is `EQUIVOCATION`; lower is `ROLLBACK`. A higher list fully replaces the allowed set. Persist the accepted bytes, version, hash and highest checked time atomically (T9). Freshness requires `issuedAt <= trustedNow < expiresAt`, where `trustedNow` is the platform clock under an explicit accurate-clock assumption, checked against a durable time high-water mark and monotonic elapsed time; rollback or an unknown clock gives `RESET_CLOCK_UNTRUSTED`. An expired or missing list gives `RESET_KEYS_REQUIRED`: refresh and defer unseen resets. None of this pauses sharing or authorises a directory key change. The list is cached until expiry.

#### Type 7: AccountResetReceipt

```
body = u8(5) || u8(7) || B(accountName) || oldIdentityId[32] || newIdentityId[32] || resetId[16] || issuedAt:u64 || authorityKeyId[16]
wire = body || Ed25519(resetKey, C("reset-sig") || body)
```

Old and new ids must differ. For an unseen receipt the SDK requires a fresh key list, an allowed `authorityKeyId`, a valid signature under that key, the account name binding, an old identity that matches its current pin, and the new identity derived from a verified new `AK`-signed certificate (`RESET_BINDING` otherwise). `issuedAt` is diagnostic: an unseen receipt under a key the current list no longer allows is `RESET_KEY_REVOKED` whatever its date. Already committed resets stay committed; the SDK retains the accepted receipt hash and the immutable (`resetId`, `accountName`, old, new) binding, a duplicate is a no-op, and a conflicting binding for a known `resetId` is rejected. The host may re-attest a legitimate old event under a current key with the same binding.

**Host.** `resetAccount(expectedOldIdentityId, newCertificate, newVault, resetId, reauthProof)` registers the new identity and device, stores the vault and the receipt, fences old writes, and tombstones every circle owned by the old identity, atomically. The reset vault starts with zero circles and a fresh `recoveryKey`, saved through the recovery provider before the reset is reported complete. The client persists the new roots, the exact certificate and vault and the `resetId` before sending (T10) and completes the reset only from them. A repeated `resetId` returns `ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the receipt before any identity check, since a device retrying after a lost response finds its old identity already replaced. **Peers.** On a validated receipt each peer records `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED`, stops sharing to the old identity, rotates affected epochs, and treats circles owned by the old identity as closed. The receipt itself is the closure proof. A bare directory key change without a receipt is quarantined, never accepted and never used to close anything.

### 13. Client contract

`open({ accountName, retentionPolicy, recoveryProvider, resetTrust, secretStore, store, transport, events })` loads protected local state and returns one asynchronous client. `retentionPolicy` is the profile's versioned policy: the global windows `deliveryWindowMs`, `appendRetryWindowMs`, `hygieneRotationMs`, `maxOutboxRecords` and `maxOutboxBytes`, and `kinds`, one declaration per record kind the product uses (section 9: `kind`, `retentionMs`, `queue`, `history`, `newest`, `headerClass`); a missing or incomplete policy is rejected and the SDK never substitutes defaults. `resetTrust` carries the pinned management key and minimum list version. `recoveryProvider` is required when the profile enables identity continuity and may be omitted on a recovery-only profile, which then has no vault at all: none is built at enrollment, replacement or reset, none is published, the directory holds none for the account (`registerAccount` and `resetAccount` take no vault), and `recoveryStatus()` reports `unsupported`, and `recoveryCode()` is `NOT_AUTHORIZED` wherever the profile does not enable identity continuity or the client holds no vault, since the code would open nothing; the SDK never asks a product for a stand-in provider. Public operations return typed results, never plaintext fallbacks. Randomness is internal in production. Adapters pass opaque bytes through unchanged. The host owns login, notifications, sensing, OS permissions and the interface; no plaintext payload ever enters the directory or relay API.

#### Public surface

- `initializeAccount()` creates the account, its first device and vault and saves the `recoveryKey` through the provider; it returns without a recovery code. `recoveryStatus()` reports the provider's `backupState`; `recoveryCode()` reveals the code on demand after local authentication.
- `restoreAccount(optionalCode)` recovers the account by identity continuity and activates a fresh device under it, trying the provider first; `NOT_AUTHORIZED` on a profile that does not enable continuity.
- `prepareReplacement()` and `completeReplacement({ successionId, authorization })` are account recovery (section 4): the proposal the product takes to the host's fresh authentication, and the commit under its token; `NOT_AUTHORIZED` on a profile that does not enable account recovery. `keyChanges({ accountId?, limit? })` reads the key changes this device accepted for its contacts, newest first and bounded; the history is never returned whole through `inspect()`, which reports the enabled replacement model, a pending proposal (its id, expected generation, whether it is stale and the generation that conflicted) and bootstrap progress.
- `enqueue(kind, payload, options?)`: a durable outbound record of a declared kind, with an opaque payload the SDK never reads. `options.eventTime` is the product's event time for the record, carried in the envelope. `options.idempotencyKey`, a caller-chosen string of at most 128 characters derived from the sample itself, carries this guarantee: a keyed enqueue that returns has committed its record and its key in one transaction (T6); any later or concurrent enqueue under the same key, from a retry after a crash or from another runtime on the same store, returns that record with `duplicate: true` and seals nothing, because the store's writer lock orders the calls and the second one finds the key; a refused enqueue stores no key, so its retry is checked again; the key is kept for `deliveryWindowMs`, after which the same key is a new hand-over; and the key covers the hand-over, not delivery, so the record it names can still be cancelled by a rotation, reported as `cancelled` like any other. `newest(identityId, kind)` reads an author's newest record of a kind with a slot, the sender's own included. One event, `recordStored { recordKind, authorIdentityId, recordId, tookSlot }`, reports every record stored, received or the device's own, with whether it took its kind's slot; `refresh { reason }` asks the product for a fresh record of each kind with a slot after a rotation or when a sent record's acceptance is in doubt. Payload codecs are the product's: the SDK ships none.
- `sync(options?)` first finishes a pending reset, activation or committed replacement, then bootstrap after account recovery (page by page, reported as `bootstrap { circles, pending, failed }` while it runs), then publishes pending owner snapshots and vault updates, then verifies metadata (certificates and succession chains, snapshots, the reset-key list when due), pushes pending sharing preferences, reconciles epochs, drains the outbox, reads and acknowledges batches. The report's `metadata.successions` counts the successions accepted. Reading is bounded: `options.catchUp` gives the records and milliseconds one sync may spend across every stream (the profile's defaults otherwise), each stream reads at least one page and acknowledges after each, and `inbox.hasMore` in the report says a stream still has records on the relay, so the host calls `sync` again at once instead of waiting for the next interval. Concurrent callers coalesce. Every step a sync worked around is one entry of the report's `errors`, with the operation, the protocol code and whether a retry may succeed; a failed download names its stream as the `inbox` operation, with the stream id and the author's identity id attached so the host can tell which sender is affected (identifying metadata under section 14: kept local or pseudonymised before logging), that stream is not counted among the streams synced, and the other streams are still read. A report with an empty `errors` means every step ran.
- `receive(wire)` for a record pushed by the host outside `sync`. `historyPage(query)` reads history in time order through the history index (section 11): an author, a kind, a `sentAt` window, ascending or descending, a page size and a cursor that names a fixed position, so pages stay stable while records arrive or expire; `history(query)` is its first page as a list and `newestEntry(query)` its newest entry. `inspect()` returns public metadata only.
- `circles.create()`, `requestJoin(circleId, ownerIdentityId, { shareOnApproval })`, `approve(circleId, accountName)`, `remove(circleId, memberIdentityId)`, `leave(circleId)`, `setEnabled(circleId, bool)`, `closeCircle(circleId)`. Device replacement never calls any of these.
- `resetAccount({ confirmed: true })`. `subscribe(listener)` for state and events. `close()` refuses every new operation (`CLOSED`), lets the operations in flight finish their current transaction and a running sync stop at its next phase boundary, then clears the listeners so nothing is emitted afterwards; it closes nothing it did not open, and reads still answer. `sync({ skipMaintenance: true })` leaves the retention purge, the periodic reset-key-list re-check and the recovery-key re-save to a later sync (a stale list is still fetched), for a short background window, and the report says so; every report carries `timings`, wall-clock milliseconds per phase and record bytes moved, within section 14's safe-log rules. `syncUntilDrained({ catchUp, maxRounds, maxMs, skipMaintenance })` runs rounds of `sync` until the relay holds no more records within the budget and bootstrap is complete, bounded by rounds (10) and time (30 seconds), and returns the merged report with the round count, why it stopped (`drained`, `maxRounds`, `maxMs`, `noProgress` when a round changed nothing and repeated its errors, `metadata` when progress needs what the host does not serve, `authentication` when it needs an authorization or a sign-in, `incomplete` when a round could not read its inbox fully so emptiness is not established, `closed`) and what remains (records on the relay, or `"unknown"`, circles to adopt, authors not yet rotated); the time left is each round's reading budget, and a transport call already in flight ends on its own timeout. `replaceDevice({ authorize, maxAttempts })` is account recovery (section 4) as one call: an earlier proposal is settled through the chain first, the same proposal is kept through an unknown outcome and a refused authorization, a new one is prepared only after a definitive conflict and only by the next call, and the product supplies only how an authorization is obtained; it returns `activated`, `pending`, `conflict` or `reauthenticate`. An adapter package may add an erasure of one account from a device: every secret the SDK keeps (its names are published as `SECRET_NAMES`, the account's roots first and the storage key last, so an interrupted erasure leaves a store that refuses to open rather than roots that would re-enroll) and the store with its journal, idempotent, nothing of another account and never the platform's synced recovery item.

#### Transport contract (implemented by the host)

- Directory: `registerAccount(certificate, vault)` (`ALREADY_COMMITTED` when the name already carries exactly this certificate, so an interrupted enrollment resumes), `resolveAccounts(names)`, `resolveIdentities(ids)` (a snapshot names account ids; the directory maps an account id, a current identity or a retired one back to the account, host-trusted like first contact, and attaches the reset receipt when the key changed through a reset, or the first page of the succession chain with a certificate under the root key when it changed through successions), `getCertificate(hash)`, `activateDevice(expectedCertificateHash, expectedVaultRevision, expectedVaultHash, newCertificate, newVault)`, `getVault(accountName)`, `publishVault(expectedRevision, expectedHash, newVault)`, `getCircle(circleId)`, `publishOwnedCircle(circleId, expectedRevision, expectedHash, newSnapshot)`, `requestJoin(circleId)`, `requestLeave(circleId)`, `readRequests(circleId)`, `getResetKeyList()`, `resetAccount(...)`, `getResetReceipt(resetId)`; and for account recovery `succeedAccount(expectedCertificateHash, newCertificate, newVault?, successionId, authorization)` returning `COMMITTED | ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the type 9 document, `CONFLICT` with the current certificate, `UNAUTHORIZED` or `RETRYABLE`; `getSuccessions(accountId, afterGeneration, limit)`, one page of the chain in generation order with `nextGeneration` when more remain, links re-attested under a current key where needed; `listCircles(cursor)`, one page of the authenticated account's circles with the sharing preference; and `setSharingPreference(circleId, enabled, expectedRevision)`, compare-and-swap on the per-circle preference revision, returning the new revision or `CONFLICT` with the current value and revision. Host rules: a retired identity's device is refused every directory mutation, every relay append and every subscription read by the certificate fence; the current-device pointer is the one source of "current"; every write of a replacement (the succession, the certificate, the device id, the spent authorization) is conditional on the compare-and-swap having moved the account, so a losing proposal leaves nothing behind and can never later answer `ALREADY_COMMITTED`; the host enforces `successionId` uniqueness and single-use authorizations; successions are logged per account and served with the chain, as evidence, not detection (section 15). How the host issues an authorization is the product's; the reference host's endpoint for it is a test stand-in.
- Relay: `getCurrentEpoch(streamId)`, `getEpoch(streamId, epoch)` returning the manifest plus the caller's wrap, `publishEpoch(streamId, expectedEpoch, expectedHash, manifest, wrapMap)`, `appendRecord(recordId, wire)`, `readRecords(streamId, afterSeq, limit)`, `ackThrough(streamId, cursor)`. The host creates a reader's subscription to a stream at admission, at device activation and when an author opens a new stream; the client never asks for one. The transport's `send` MUST be callable from inside T6h without awaiting.
- Notifications are the host's, decided from the record header class as the product declares it: in the first profile a visible notification to the owner on a join request and to each recipient on a class 1 append; a silent wake-up on a class 0 append; nothing on a class 2 append. The SDK never composes notification text.

**Transport error contract.** A transport raises `NETWORK`, retryable, for every failure to reach the host or to get its answer: no connection, a timeout, a 5xx, a 408, and a connection lost while the response body is still being read, since the fetch promise resolves at the headers. A 429 is `RATE_LIMITED`, retryable, with `retryAfterMs` taken from `Retry-After` (seconds or an HTTP date) when the host sent one, so a client backs off for at least that long instead of retrying on its ordinary schedule. A 404 is an absent object (`undefined` from a read), never an error. An answer that arrived but is not JSON is the host's bug and a plain error, not `NETWORK`, so the client reports it as unexpected and not retryable. A transport never retries a mutation itself: the client re-enters its own retry rules with the exact persisted bytes. The reference HTTP transport's failure test (`examples/host-worker/test/transport.test.ts`) is the conformance test for this contract.

Mutation outcomes are `COMMITTED`, `ALREADY_COMMITTED` (same bytes), `CONFLICT` (with the current bytes), `IGNORED_OLDER`, `RETRYABLE`, `UNAUTHORIZED` or `PERMANENT_FAILURE`. Timeouts are uncertain: re-read the target and compare bytes. Use a 30-second timeout and a uniform random delay of 0 to min(60 s, 2^min(attempt, 6) s) between attempts, one pending operation per target. A retry re-enters T6h like a first send.

### 14. States, errors and logs

**Visible states:** `ready`, `locked`, `accountRecoveryRequired`, `replacementPrepared`, `activationPending`, `retired`, `paused(sync | rotation | metadata | limit)`, `quarantined`. `ready` means local encryption state is usable, not that the server disclosed globally fresh membership. States are emitted only after the corresponding durable transition. A device change is not a member-left or member-joined event.

**Error families.** Parsing: `SIZE_LIMIT`, `MALFORMED`, `UNSUPPORTED_VERSION`, `UNSUPPORTED_TYPE`, `BAD_PAYLOAD`. Authentication: `INVALID_KEY`, `BAD_SIGNATURE`, `DECRYPT_FAILED`, `WRAP_HASH_MISMATCH`, `KEY_COMMITMENT_MISMATCH`. Trust and state: `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED`, `ACCOUNT_RECOVERY_REQUIRED`, `DEVICE_ROLLBACK`, `DEVICE_ID_REUSED`, `SUPERSEDED_DEVICE`, `VAULT_BINDING`, `RESET_BINDING`, `RESET_KEYS_REQUIRED`, `RESET_KEY_REVOKED`, `RESET_CLOCK_UNTRUSTED`, `SUCCESSION_BINDING`, `SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`, `AUTHORIZATION_REQUIRED`, `AUTHORIZATION_EXPIRED`, `REPLACEMENT_CONFLICT`, `SUCCESSION_PENDING` (retryable), `ROLLBACK`, `EQUIVOCATION`, `METADATA_REQUIRED`, `KEY_PENDING`, `NOT_AUTHORIZED`, `ROTATION_REQUIRED`, `COUNTER_EXHAUSTED`, `RECIPIENT_LIMIT`. Delivery: `CLOCK_SKEW`, `EXPIRED`, `QUEUE_FULL`, `CURSOR_OUT_OF_RANGE`, `STALE_APPEND`. Storage and transport: `LOCKED`, `CLOSED` (an operation after `close()`), `RECOVERY_REQUIRED`, `STORAGE_FAILURE`, `NETWORK`, `RATE_LIMITED` (retryable, with the host's wait) plus the mutation outcomes. A transport raises `NETWORK`, which is retryable, for every failure to reach the host or to get an answer in time (connection errors, timeouts, server errors), so the client can tell a temporary loss of connectivity from a programming error, which it reports as unexpected and not retryable. Each error carries a retryable flag and a safe stage name. A wrong recovery secret fails authentication and never triggers a reset.

**Safe logs.** Allowed fields: a random operation id, stage and result code, recovery provider status and reason, reset-key list version and expiry, device generation, circle and vault revision, epoch and counter, counts, byte lengths, duration and retry count. Never log payloads, plaintext, seeds, `SK`, recovery codes, store keys, session tokens or vault plaintext. Account, circle and record ids are identifying metadata: keep them local or pseudonymise per session. Ciphertext and diagnostic exports are never uploaded automatically. There is no flag that disables verification.

### 15. Security properties and limits

**What is protected.** Payload confidentiality against the server and against non-members, under pinned keys: the server never holds `recoveryKey`, `accountSeed`, `deviceSeed` or any `SK`. Record authenticity and author binding through the `DSK` signature and the certificate chain to the pinned `AK`. Membership authenticity through owner signatures over complete snapshots. Continuity across device replacement through stable `AK` pinning and generation high-water marks, with the replaced device cut off as each author rotates. Rollback and equivocation detection for certificates, snapshots, epochs, records and reset-key lists. Account login alone cannot forge an `AK`-authorised replacement device. Holding the vault and its recovery key authorises a new device, and with it future access; it decrypts no history.

| Attacker | Can | Cannot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Honest-but-curious server | See who shares with whom, when, how often, record sizes, header classes and `sentAt`; infer approximate whereabouts from network metadata | Read any payload, event time or kind |
| Malicious server | Substitute a key at an account's first contact; withhold removals, snapshots, certificates, vaults, key lists or records indefinitely; replay an old vault to a recovering device or an unexpired key list for its remaining lifetime; fork views between members; show an owner a join request the named account never made | Produce a document that verifies under a pinned key; roll back a generation, revision, epoch or list version a client has seen; decrypt anything |
| Removed member | Keep the plaintext and keys it already holds | Open any epoch published after the author learned of the removal |
| Former device of a member | Keep its own old history; keep opening the epochs it already holds keys for until each author rotates to the new device | Open any wrap addressed to the replacement device; publish as the account after the new certificate is known |
| Holder of a recovery code or `accountSeed` | Authorise a device of their own and act as the account, visibly, through a new certificate | Read anything without publishing that certificate; this is root compromise, and only reset ends it |
| Holder of an allowed reset key, continuity-only deployment | Retire identities and close their circles until the key is removed from the list | Admit a member, decrypt a record, or substitute a decrypting key |
| Holder of an allowed authority key, deployment enabling account recovery | Sign a succession for any account to keys it holds and inherit everything that account can do: read its **future** traffic from each peer's next rotation; and, when the account owns a circle, sign that circle's next snapshots, admit a member of its choosing, remove members or close the circle, and so read that circle's future traffic through the admitted member. It cannot forge the original owner's signature; it authorizes a successor with the same powers | Obtain any historical epoch key, or by itself decrypt ciphertext encrypted only under earlier epochs; prevent every peer's durable key-change record; sign two successors for one state without producing evidence of equivocation |
| Another member | Copy and export what it legitimately received | Forge records as someone else |

#### Limits, stated plainly

1. Enrollment is host-trusted, and so are join requests: the owner admits the account a request names under the key the directory serves. The guarantee is continuity of an accepted identity while local trust state survives, subject to the declared recovery paths.
2. Removal takes effect per author, when that author learns of it and rotates. A withholding server delays it without bound; the client keeps sharing with the last verified snapshot and shows its age.
3. Replacement revokes the old device the same way, per author and on each author's next sync. Until an author rotates, an unlocked old device can still open that author's current epoch.
4. No forward secrecy and no post-compromise recovery. Independent epoch keys limit one key's exposure to its epoch; a compromised current device exposes what it holds.
5. Recovery trusts the server's latest vault. An existing device detects a rolled-back vault from its checkpoints; a fresh device cannot. The vault holds no decryption key, so a rolled-back vault costs circle pins and preferences, not plaintext; a restored old sharing preference re-enables sharing only to members the owner admitted.
6. The reset authority can deny service until a key is removed from the list; a client that never syncs never learns the removal. Compromise of the management key requires an app update.
7. A whole-device backup restored on a device with no surviving freshness anchor cannot always be recognised as old. This is not anti-rollback protection.
8. Metadata, authorised-device leaks, the truth of what a device senses and the screens of members are outside payload confidentiality.
9. Deletion on the server is a conforming-host policy the client cannot verify; the client's own deletion is what it controls.
10. Delivery and notifications are availability properties, not cryptographic ones. A withholding server can delay a record; it cannot forge one.
11. On a deployment that enables account recovery the precise guarantee is: a succession transfers no historical epoch key and by itself enables no decryption of ciphertext encrypted only under earlier epochs; a malicious host cannot substitute a key without leaving a durable record on every peer that verified the substitution, and cannot sign two successors for one state without producing evidence that proves it; it can substitute a key, and through an owner it can change membership, and both take effect at each peer's next rotation. The succession log is evidence, not detection: a host can serve one branch to one peer and another to another, and detecting a split view needs comparison across peers or an independent monitor, which this specification does not define. Compromise of an operational key exposes future traffic of every account it is used against until the key is removed from the list and the successions are noticed; the 30-day list lifetime bounds the first, not the second. A product that enables account recovery, alone or alongside continuity, says so in its profile and in its user-facing security description, because the weaker enabled model sets the promise.
12. Bootstrap after account recovery trusts the host for the currency of the circles and preferences it serves (section 5); the bound is that the device shares only with members an owner once admitted, under keys it verifies, and that bound does not hold against the combined attacker of row 2 and the authority key, who can replace the owner and admit anyone. Discovery can omit a circle, which is withholding; it cannot forge a snapshot.

**Audit status.** Primitives are libsodium's and audited; the composition is not yet independently reviewed. The vectors in section 16 are implementation evidence, not an audit.

### 16. Conformance

The companion package is the SS5 revision 2 `vectors.json` plus `verify_vectors.py`: a reproducible reference codec and small state model, not a production SDK. The Kavach v1 fixture in `spec/` is regenerated once from it, by a checker derived from the same source, with four changes: the maximum key-list lifetime is 30 days instead of 24 hours, so case K19 is re-derived at 30 days plus one millisecond; record flag bit 0 is reserved rather than meaning urgent, which leaves the core record untouched and re-derives only the maximum-size record, whose flags move from 1 to 2 (a kind 1 record carrying an opaque 4,096-byte payload), with a new negative vector for bit 0; type 5 is retired, so its document vector is dropped and its wire joins the negative set as `UNSUPPORTED_TYPE`; and the vault entry lost its snapshot pins, so the vault vectors are re-derived with the 49-byte entry and the transfer token is gone. Every other section applies byte for byte, including the two core sealed boxes, the 100-account set, the device replacement set and the reset-key management cases other than K19. Revision 8 regenerates every record vector once more: the plaintext under the AEAD is the envelope followed by the payload, the header's class bits replace the kind bits, the maximum-size record grows to 4,265 bytes, the length negative moves to 4,123, and the two-kind-bits negative becomes an envelope-version negative checked after decryption. Revision 9 adds without changing: a type 9 vector signed by the fixture's operational key for the fixture's Alice, with the generation 2 certificate under a new account key, asserted at 251 bytes plus the name; a re-attestation, a fork, a return to the retired identity and a gap, each with its certificate; four negatives (a generation that is not previous plus one, equal identities, a zero id, a wrong signer); and ten verifier cases (C01 to C10): acceptance; the same attestation again as `DUPLICATE`; a re-attestation as the same succession; two successors for one state as `EQUIVOCATION`; a return to a retired identity as `SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`; identity-continuity activations between successions accepted; a rollback below the pinned generation; a certificate that does not bind as `SUCCESSION_BINDING`; a new attestation under a removed key as `RESET_KEY_REVOKED`; and an accepted transition recognised after its key left the list. The storage-row fixture is informative only.

**What the fixture contains.** Five core signed documents spanning types 1 to 4 and 7; two sealed key boxes with their wrap hashes; a record whose plaintext is the envelope (kind 0, with an event time) and the first profile's 22-byte payload, with its AAD, ciphertext, signature and record id; the 100-account boundary set (snapshot 3,324 bytes, manifest 9,808 bytes, wraps 8,000 bytes, maximum record 4,265 bytes); device replacement (generation 2 certificate, unchanged snapshot, continued owner snapshot, new manifest and record, the old device failing to open the new wrap, superseded manifest detection); vault encryption and wrong-key rejection; durable event records surviving a later record of a slot kind; a slot-only record with header class 2 and no event time; an account reset receipt; an account succession with its successor certificate and the four related documents; three key lists with 22 reset-key management cases and 10 succession cases; 26 negative byte vectors; 3 wrap and key checks; 17 state-model transitions. Ed25519 and X25519 outputs are cross-checked with OpenSSL, XChaCha20-Poly1305 with PyCryptodome; sealed boxes use libsodium on both sides and are not an independent cross-check.

#### Required tests beyond the checker

- Run the fixture on every supported platform; compare complete bytes, not round trips. Fuzz bounded parsers with a 16 KiB limit. Exercise every boundary: names 1/64/65, payloads 0/4096/4097, counts 0/1/100/101, integer maxima, duplicate and unsorted rows, reserved flags, non-canonical signatures, invalid points, wrong key commitments, vault binding, key-list rollback and expiry.
- Crash on both sides of T1 to T10 and T6h and at every lost-response point, against the real store. Race execution contexts. Reopen and assert no reused counter, no reconstructed nonce, no stale-audience send, exact-byte retry.
- Lost responses and refused calls: each directory and relay mutation with its response lost once after the host committed, and each refused once before reaching it; enrollment, activation, owner snapshots, vault updates and reset all converge on the next sync, with the store's bytes compared against the host's afterwards.
- The handoff race: an authorization change committed by one context while another is between eligibility check and send never results in an upload of obsolete ciphertext, tested with the real transport adapter.
- Lost device, two tests. Device keys only: the attacker holds `deviceSeed` and the store but not the account root; after the new device activates and every author has rotated, it opens nothing new. Full state: the attacker also holds `accountSeed` or `recoveryKey` with the vault; the test shows it can still authorise a device of its own, so replacement is not a repair and only reset ends it.
- Acknowledgement: a `keyPending` record never advances the prefix while later records still flow; the relay never deletes a record while a required reader's cursor is below it; an equal or older cursor causes no write; a cursor above the tail is rejected; a reader is returned only records addressed to its certificate.
- Relay: deliver counters 7 then 6 and keep the slot at 7; the record disappears when the last required reader's cursor passes it and at the window without one; expiry advances as a prefix and never expires a newer record; a blocked stream never delays another stream; a retry inside the window returns the original outcome after deletion; an append below the checkpoint without evidence is `STALE_APPEND` and the client marks it `acceptanceUnknown` without re-creating the record; a new device's subscription starts at activation and excludes records sealed to the old certificate; every member gets a subscription when an author opens a new stream; epoch blobs disappear only when no record references them and the epoch is not current; an event record survives a later record of a slot kind end to end.
- Client reports and queries: a download that fails for one stream is one entry of the sync report and the other streams are still read; the same idempotency key from a retry or a second runtime yields one record and a new key a new one; history pages read through the index keep their order and cursor while records arrive and expire, and a store from before the index reads the same after its backfill.
- Reset: the 22 key-management cases against the real clock adapter, plus wrong key, revoked key with a backdated receipt, binding mismatch, duplicate, conflicting binding, receipt without a verified certificate, expired list deferring a reset without pausing sharing, and no closure on a bare directory key change.
- Account recovery: scenarios 3 and 4 repeated with no recovery material; owners and peers missing several successions; a member succession while the owner is offline; removed, departed, pending, disabled and closed circles across a succession; two replacements racing for one account; crashes and lost responses around T11 and the commit, including a restart that finishes from the chain; the old device refused after the commit; a succession by a key the list never allowed, refused by the replacing device and by every peer; discovery pages interrupted and resumed; the lost device with the complete old state obtaining no succession without the product's authentication; a continuity-only deployment refusing a served succession and rotating nothing; mixed transitions; an owner's succession with the host refusing the retired key, a member bootstrapping in the window verifying the retired-key snapshot, and the republication; a long-offline peer across an authority-key rotation, with and without re-attestation; a crash between acceptance and the event; the bootstrap limit with a pre-removal snapshot; succession, then reset, with a receipt forged to name the retired key; the composition of both models ending with the original account id and the chain as evidence; chain paging with the account out of every audience until the last page; and preference ordering, including a late success for an older request. These run over the fake host, and the replacement flow also over HTTP against the reference Worker.

An independent review of the composed protocol, the recovery flows, the store binding, the handoff serialisation and the host's atomicity contract precedes production. Passing the checker is not that review.

#### Revision 5

Revision 5 changes no wire byte and no vector. It corrects the durable-state rules that the first implementation and its fault harness exposed: promotion is idempotent and a pending epoch is never overwritten; pending owner snapshots and vault updates are published by every sync; the own reset is persisted before it is sent and the host answers a repeated `resetId` before any identity check; receipt acceptance commits with its effects; a completed approval keeps the newer sharing preference; a circle with no other member closes; the recovery-key save is retried on the first sync; an interrupted enrollment resumes on `ALREADY_COMMITTED`; and a record whose epoch the host served unverifiable is held as `keyPending` with the error named.

#### Revision 6

Revision 6 assigns flag bit 2 of the record header, reserved until now, to a third record kind (the first profile's state kind), and changes nothing else on the wire. The fixture's reserved-flag negative moves to bit 3, a negative with bits 1 and 2 both set is added, and a slot-only record vector is added; every other vector is unchanged. Readers adopt revision 6 before any sender emits the new kind, since a revision 5 reader rejects it as malformed. Revision 6 also states three client-contract rules (section 13) that need no wire change: a failed download is reported, not counted as a synced stream; history is read through a time-ordered index with stable cursors (section 11); and an enqueue may carry an idempotency key.

#### Revision 7

Revision 7 changes no wire byte and no vector. It changes one receiver rule: the newest slot is chosen by event time within a device generation (section 10), where revision 6 chose it by (epoch, counter), so a record with an earlier event time sent later no longer moves an author's newest backward. The fixture's state cases S11 to S13 and S16 still hold, since equal event times are ordered by (epoch, counter) and S16's older record is also the earlier event. It also bounds a sync's reading with a budget and `hasMore` (section 13), states the durability guarantee of a keyed enqueue, keeps the sender's own slots under the reader's rules (section 10), and moves the behaviour of each record kind out of the protocol into the product's declaration (section 9): retention, queue semantics, history and the newest slot are per kind in the policy, the two named history windows are gone, and the slots of every kind are one table.

#### Revision 8

Revision 8 changes the record plaintext and regenerates the record vectors (section 16); the header bytes and every other document are unchanged. An envelope of ten bytes precedes the payload under the AEAD: a version, the kind, and the event time the sender gave. The kind therefore leaves the header, whose two bits now carry the kind's declared header class, so a product may use up to 256 kinds and show the relay only what it chooses; both class bits set is legal. The newest slot orders by the envelope's event time, clamped to `sentAt`, in place of a field of the first profile's payload, so the SDK no longer reads any payload. With this revision the specification names no record kind: the first product's kinds and codecs moved to its profile. The payload maximum stays 4,096 bytes; the ciphertext bounds move to 26 and 4,122. A revision 7 reader cannot open a revision 8 record, so a deployment moves every device before any sender emits one.

#### Revision 9

Revision 9 adds document type 9, the account succession, and changes no existing byte or vector; it adopts amendment A1. An account now has an account id, the identity at enrollment, and circles, manifests, streams and records name accounts by it, so a key change through a succession renames nothing. A profile declares the replacement models it enables, and peers enforce the declaration: on a continuity-only deployment every type 9 document is `UNSUPPORTED_TYPE` and a changed key stays quarantined. The client gains `prepareReplacement`, `completeReplacement`, `keyChanges`, bootstrap through the host's circle listing with a host-held sharing preference ordered by a revision, and the states and codes section 14 lists; the directory gains `succeedAccount`, `getSuccessions`, `listCircles` and `setSharingPreference`, serves chains with a certificate under the root key, re-attests links whose signer left the list, and fences a retired device's reads as well as its writes. A reset after successions retires the whole chain. The retired device of a succession erases its account seed. One simplification against the amendment's text is stated here so that nobody reads more into the implementation: the reference host's authorization endpoint is a test stand-in for the product's fresh authentication, not a design for it. Two reviews of the implementation added rules that this text now carries: the first-contact policy gate and verification, retired owner keys only at revision 0, the host's conditional replacement writes, retried bootstrap circles, resumable budgeted chains, one transition validator for every path with reconciliation of the accepted prefix, idempotent stream opening as the commit's completion step, no vault on a recovery-only profile without a provider, re-attestation of a cached prefix after an authority rotation, resumption of a saved cursor whatever the prefix, authentication before any claim of equivocation, no recovery code where none can be used, the whole own chain kept at activation, self-retirement failures reported, the chain bound applied inside the one traversal, and the Expo helper's provider optional like core's.

### 17. Questions for the reviewer

1. Is the stable identity (`accountName`, `AK`) plus a per-device certificate the right unit, or should readers also pin the device id to detect a host that swaps devices between legitimate generations?
2. The manifest binds each recipient's certificate hash. Is anything lost by not binding the recipient's device generation as well?
3. Host-trusted enrollment with optional fingerprint comparison, and host-attested join requests with no requester signature: acceptable for v1, or should an authenticated exchange be required for owners at least?
4. Vault recovery under a random 256-bit key with no password, and no old-device transfer path: is the lack of a vault freshness anchor on a fresh device acceptable given that the vault grants account control and future access but decrypts no history?
5. Reset-key lists with a 30-day maximum lifetime: is the window between a key compromise and the next list a tolerable denial-of-service exposure, and is the trusted-clock assumption acceptable on phones?
6. Deletion when the last required reader's cursor passes a record, with one expiry prefix per stream: any attack the cumulative cursors or the acceptance-time ordering expose?
7. The acknowledgement rule holds the prefix at a `keyPending` record while newer records are stored, and the relay filters by subscription. Is there a sequence where a reader acknowledges and still lacks plaintext it needs?
8. A newest slot advances on (generation, event time, epoch, counter) while history accepts out-of-order counters. Is there a replay that moves a slot forward illegitimately?
9. A cross-process lock with the send call initiated inside it as the only serialisation of the handoff: sufficient on both mobile platforms' background execution models, and is a send that fails synchronously handled safely?
10. Every kind shares the record counter and differs only inside the envelope, with two header bits of class. Should a product's event kinds have their own stream instead?
11. What should the first external audit cover beyond the composition: the recovery flows, the host atomicity, or the platform adapters?
12. Account recovery makes the host's operational key a key-substitution key with an owner's powers (section 15, item 11), bounded by durable evidence rather than prevention. Is evidence without a defined detection path an acceptable basis for a product to enable it, and what monitoring should a profile be required to run?

### Appendix. Scenario tests that gate release

Each scenario is an executable test against the real client with a fake host; the relay scenarios also run against the host's real relay. They gate the delivery and replacement milestones of the build sequence.

1. Create a circle, request a join, approve it: the owner is notified once; the requester's first manifest names both devices; the owner's audience rotates once.
2. Approve a join while the requester is offline: on its return the requester enables sharing from its vault preference without a second approval.
3. Owner replaces device with a member offline: the circle revision is unchanged; the owner signs revision plus one afterwards; the member rotates to the new device on return.
4. Member replaces device while the owner is offline: each online author rotates to the new `DEK` within one sync without owner involvement; the old device cannot open the new wraps.
5. Lost device with full old-device state in the attacker's hands: after activation and every author's rotation the attacker opens nothing new; the test records what it could open before the last rotation.
6. Two candidate devices activate concurrently: exactly one commits; the other reads the committed state and reports it.
7. Lose the local store, keep recovery: membership survives; the new stream starts at epoch 1, counter 0; no old counter is reused.
8. Enrollment with the recovery provider unavailable: the account is created, sharing works, recovery status reports not backed up, and the code is reachable later.
9. Platform recovery to a second device with no old device and no code; a wrong platform account yields `unavailable`, never a new account.
10. Both recovery paths fail: `ACCOUNT_RECOVERY_REQUIRED`; circles remain intact; nothing closes.
11. Reset with a valid receipt under an allowed key: peers close owned circles and re-admit the new identity only after owner approval; the same receipt under a key removed by a newer list is `RESET_KEY_REVOKED`; a bare directory key change closes nothing; an expired list defers the reset and does not pause sharing.
12. Removal with the owner online: the author pauses, rotates and the removed member cannot open the next epoch.
13. Removal withheld by the server: the author keeps sharing with the last verified snapshot and shows its age.
14. Remove then rejoin between two syncs: `desiredRevision` still increments and the epoch rotates.
15. Over-limit union across circles: `RECIPIENT_LIMIT`, nothing truncated.
16. Crash after T4 and before publish: restart publishes identical bytes. Crash after remote commit and before T5: lookup promotes without new randomness.
17. Membership changes while an epoch is in flight: the epoch commits obsolete; enqueue returns `ROTATION_REQUIRED`; the next epoch promotes.
18. Crash after T6 and before send: restart retries the exact wire; crash after a membership change cancels it with a disposition.
19. Account recovery of a member with no recovery material: the proposal, the host's succession, bootstrap of its circles with the preference it had, every peer rotating to the new key under the same account id, the old device retired with its account seed erased and its reads refused.
20. Account recovery of an owner: the served snapshot republished at the next revision under the new key, members verifying the republication after the owner's chain, and the successor keeping its power over membership.
21. Succession, then reset: the receipt names the current key, every circle the account id owns closes at every peer, and a receipt forged to name the retired key is `RESET_BINDING`.
22. The composition of both models: enrollment, a succession, a bootstrap with a fresh vault, then identity continuity on a third device ending with the original account id, the same circles and the chain as evidence.
19. Handoff race: a membership change committed by a background context while the foreground sender is between its check and its send never produces an upload sealed for the old audience.
20. Deliver counters 7 then 6: both in history, the slot stays at 7.
21. Record arrives while offline before its epoch could be fetched: `keyPending`, the acknowledged prefix holds, later records are stored, the epoch is fetched on the next sync and the record is read again and opened; the relay still held it.
22. Last recipient acknowledges: the record is gone from the relay; the epoch blobs are gone once the epoch is not current and nothing references it.
23. A recipient stays away past the window: the record is deleted unacknowledged; the author's history still has it.
24. A record of an event kind, then two of a slot kind: three records delivered; the event survives on relay and reader; the slot holds the last record; recipients were notified by header class, visibly for the event and silently for the others.
25. Two execution contexts enqueue concurrently: counters are unique and the outbox order is preserved.
26. Hygiene rotation at the configured interval and counter exhaustion: both produce a new epoch and `refresh`.
27. Superseded device sends after its replacement activated: the relay refuses; a reader that receives it anyway stores history only and keeps the slot.
28. An approval whose publication completes after the user disabled sharing: sharing stays disabled and the next epoch names no member.
29. A removal, close, preference change or reset whose publication failed or whose response was lost: the next sync completes it, and a replacement device restores the result, vault included.
30. A peer that dies between accepting a reset receipt and acting on it: after restart the circles the old identity owned are closed and its pin is gone.
