# Amendment A1: account replacement authorized by the host's signing authority

Status: **draft for review, revision 2, not adopted**. Nothing in the SDK, the hosts or the fixture implements it. Revision 2 answers the first review's seven points; section 10 lists them. It proposes protocol revision 9 on wire version 5. Section numbers refer to `spec/Kavach-v1.md` at revision 8. Where this document and the specification disagree today, the specification wins; if the amendment is adopted, its text replaces the sections it names.

## 0. What this changes, in one paragraph

Today a replacement device proves it is the same account by holding the account key, recovered through the platform's synced secret store or the recovery code (section 4, "Replacing the device"). This amendment adds a second way: a new device with **no secret from the old one** generates fresh account and device keys, and the host's signing authority, after the product's fresh authentication, signs a **succession** that binds the account to the new keys. Peers verify the succession and rotate, exactly as they do for a device change today; nobody approves anything; circle ids, membership, pending joins, ownership and closures are unchanged; the old device is fenced; the new device receives no historical keys. The price is stated in section 6 and is the reason this is a decision and not a patch: an authority that can sign successions can bind any account to keys of its choosing, and through an owner's account it inherits that owner's power over membership.

## 2. Identity model

### 2a. The two replacement models

| Model | How the new device proves it is the account | Who must be trusted | Name in this document |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Host-independent identity continuity** | It holds the account key, recovered through the platform's synced secret store or the recovery code (section 4, "Replacing the device", unchanged) | Nobody beyond first contact: the host cannot rebind a key | identity continuity |
| **Convenient account recovery** | It holds nothing; the host's authority signs a succession after the product's fresh authentication (sections 3 and 4 below) | The host's signing authority, for every account's future traffic and, through owners, for every circle's membership | account recovery |

A profile declares which models it enables: `replacement: "continuity" | "recovery" | "both"`. The declaration is a property of the app build, decided by the product and never by a user: it is compiled into every client, it has no runtime setter, no per-account form and no server-delivered value, and it changes only by shipping a new app version, as the pinned management key does. What a user ever sees is the consequence (what a replacement asks of them, and the notice when a contact's key changes), never a choice. **The declaration is enforced by the peers who verify, not only by the device being replaced.** A client whose profile does not enable account recovery rejects every type 9 document as `UNSUPPORTED_TYPE`, whoever signed it, keeps the account's key quarantined as `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED` exactly as it does today for a bare directory key change, and ignores the chain the directory attaches. That is what makes identity continuity host-independent in fact: on such a deployment a host that tries to rebind a key obtains nothing from any client. A client that enables account recovery verifies successions by section 3. A product that enables both has the convenience of both and the guarantee of account recovery, since every account on it can be rebound by the host; its profile states the enabled models and the resulting promise in one sentence, so nobody reads the stronger guarantee into a deployment that allows the weaker model.


The protocol gains one definition and changes none on the wire.

- **`accountId`** is the `identityId` an account had at enrollment: `H(C("identity") || B(accountName) || AK₀)`, where `AK₀` is its first account key. It never changes. Every document that names an account keeps naming it by `accountId`: the owner and members of a snapshot (type 2), the author and rows of a manifest (type 3), the vault header's binding, and the old identity of a reset receipt (type 7).
- **`identityId`** keeps its definition, `H(C("identity") || B(accountName) || AK)`, and is now the identity of the account's **current key**. For an account never replaced by authority, `accountId = identityId`, which is every account that exists today.
- A **succession chain** for an account is the ordered list of verified successions from `accountId` to its current `identityId`. Resolving an account means following its chain to the current key and certificate. The chain is empty for an account that has never succeeded.
- `deviceGeneration` is the **account version**. It is already monotonic per account and already advances by exactly one at every activation under compare-and-swap on the host. A succession advances it by one, like a device change under the same key. There is no second version counter.

Consequences for existing rules:

- Section 4, "First contact and pinning": a different `AK` for a pinned account remains `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED` and remains quarantined, until either a reset receipt (section 12, as today) or a verified succession (section 4 below) is processed. The rule "never on an unsigned directory change" stands.
- Section 6, snapshots: members and the owner are account ids. "Resolving members MUST reject two different account keys for one account name" becomes "two different current keys for one account id". A member's succession never changes a snapshot and the owner does not republish for it. An owner's succession does: the successor's first act in bootstrap (section 5) is to republish the current snapshot at the next revision under its new key, so the circle has a snapshot under the current owner key within one sync. Three questions are kept apart for every snapshot (the same split applies to manifests below): **validity**, the signature verifies under its signer; **authority**, that signer is a key in the owner's verified chain, current or retired; **currency**, whether the snapshot may govern membership and sharing now. A valid snapshot under any chain key is accepted as membership state subject to the revision rules; only the owner's current key may advance the revision, and the host refuses a publication signed by a retired key. Retired owner keys are therefore retained in the chain for as long as a snapshot they signed may still be verified.
- Section 7, audience: an audience entry is (`accountId`, `certificateHash`, `DEK.public`) for the account's current device under its current key, found through the chain. Deduplication and the 100-recipient limit are by account id, as today. A changed key with a verified succession is a rotation trigger (an accepted recipient key change), not a security error.
- Section 8, manifests: `authorIdentityId` and `recipientIdentityId` are account ids. The reader verifies the author's certificate under the chain key that signed it, which may be a retired key for an epoch published before a succession, and checks that the certificate's derived identity is the chain identity of that generation and its stream id matches. Only the author's current key and device may publish a new epoch; a manifest from a retired device is history only, as a superseded device's records are today (section 9, "Verification"). The reader's own row must name the reader's account id and its current certificate hash.
- The relay keys streams by `streamId`, which already changes with every device; nothing in section 10 changes.

## 3. The succession document

A new signed document, type 9, label `succession-sig`, signed by an operational key allowed by the current reset-key list (section 12). It is distinct from the reset receipt: a receipt retires an identity and closes what it owned; a succession continues everything.

```
body = u8(5) || u8(9) || B(accountName) || accountId[32]
       || previousIdentityId[32] || newIdentityId[32]
       || previousGeneration:u64 || newGeneration:u64
       || newCertificateHash[32] || successionId[16] || issuedAt:u64 || authorityKeyId[16]
wire = body || Ed25519(authorityKey, C("succession-sig") || body)
```

Rules: `newGeneration = previousGeneration + 1`; previous and new identity differ; `successionId` is random, 16 bytes, nonzero, and unique per account; `issuedAt` is diagnostic. Wire length is 283 bytes plus the name length.

**Verification by a peer**, for an unseen succession of account `accountId`:

1. A fresh reset-key list (section 12 rules, including `RESET_KEYS_REQUIRED` and `RESET_CLOCK_UNTRUSTED`), an allowed `authorityKeyId`, a valid signature under that key.
2. `accountName` and `accountId` match the local pin for that account.
3. `previousIdentityId` equals the account's current pinned identity, and `previousGeneration` is at least the pinned generation. The two may differ by identity-continuity activations the peer has not seen: under one key a higher generation with gaps is legal today (section 4, "Generation rules"), and a succession from such a generation is accepted on the same terms, with no further certificate evidence. Between successions the chain is contiguous: a succession's previous identity must be the identity the prior succession produced. A previous generation below the pinned one is `ROLLBACK`. Two valid successions with the same previous state and different bodies are `EQUIVOCATION`, which quarantines the account: the authority signed two successors for one state.
4. A verified type 1 certificate whose body hash is `newCertificateHash`, signed by the new `AK`, carrying `accountName`, `deviceGeneration = newGeneration`, a new `deviceId`, and whose derived identity is `newIdentityId` (`SUCCESSION_BINDING` otherwise, a new code in the trust family).
5. The previous identity is not already retired by a reset receipt or an earlier succession (`SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`).

On acceptance, in one transaction: pin the new `AK`, identity, generation and certificate hash for the account; retire the previous identity (its certificates verify nothing new; its stream is history only); increment `desiredRevision` (section 7); record the succession hash and the immutable (`successionId`, `accountId`, previous, new) binding, so a duplicate is a no-op and a conflicting binding for a known id is rejected; and **write a key-change history row** (`accountId`, previous and new identity, generation, `successionId`, accepted-at) in the same transaction. The row is the notice: it is durable, it is read through `inspect()` and a `keyChanges(accountId?)` query, and a product that shows key changes reads it. After commit, `accountKeyChanged { accountId, accountName, reason: "succession", generation }` tells a listener that the history changed; a crash between commit and event loses the event and nothing else, since the row is already there. The row is not optional and the client never suppresses it; what the product shows is the product's decision (section 6).

**Chains.** A peer that missed successions receives them in order from the directory (section 7 below) and verifies each against the state the previous one produced. A peer applies no succession out of order and never skips one: a gap is `METADATA_REQUIRED`, and the peer pauses sharing to that account until the chain is complete, as it does today for a missing certificate.

**Authority keys over time.** Step 1 requires a signer the current list allows, and a link signed long ago may carry a key id the list has since dropped. Three cases are kept apart. *Rotation*: the key was retired in the ordinary course; the host re-attests the link under a current allowed key with the identical binding, as section 12 already permits for receipts, and serves the re-attestation in the chain; the peer verifies the re-attestation and never the original. *Compromise*: the key was revoked because it was misused; the host MUST NOT re-attest links it cannot vouch for, and a peer that is served an unseen link under a revoked key without a re-attestation reports `RESET_KEY_REVOKED` and keeps the account quarantined; which links a host vouches for after a compromise is a host duty the protocol states and cannot verify. *History*: a link a peer verified before the key was dropped stays verified, as a committed reset stays committed today. `issuedAt` decides none of this; it is diagnostic.

## 4. Replacing the account keys (new text for section 4)

The existing procedure, "Replacing the device", is **identity continuity** and is unchanged. This is **account recovery**. A profile enables one or both (section 2a); the SDK refuses to prepare a replacement by a model the profile has not enabled, and peers refuse to verify one.

1. **Prepare** (`prepareReplacement()`, T11). On a fresh device, authenticated to the host as the account by the product's own login, the SDK resolves the account's current certificate and generation `g` through the directory, generates a fresh `accountSeed`, `deviceSeed`, `deviceId` and, when the profile uses a vault, a fresh `recoveryKey`, builds the certificate at generation `g + 1` under the new `AK`, and persists the roots, the exact certificate bytes, a random `successionId`, the expected current certificate hash and `g` in the secret store and the store, before returning. It returns the **proposal**: `{ successionId, accountName, accountId, expectedCertificateHash, expectedGeneration, certificate }`, all public. A second call on the same device returns the same pending proposal. The remote account is untouched.
2. **Authorize** (outside the SDK). The product performs its fresh authentication and asks the host to authorize the proposal. The host binds the authorization to the proposal's hash, the account, the expected generation and a single-use challenge with an expiry, and returns an opaque token. A long-lived session alone MUST NOT obtain one. How the host authenticates is the product's; the protocol sees only the token.
3. **Commit** (`completeReplacement({ successionId, authorization })`). The SDK calls `succeedAccount(expectedCertificateHash, certificate, vault?, successionId, authorization)`. The host, atomically: checks the authorization against the proposal and the challenge; compares and swaps the account's current certificate hash; stores the new certificate; signs the type 9 succession under the active operational key; moves the current-device pointer; fences the previous device from every write and from the relay; records the succession in the account's log; and returns the succession. Outcomes follow section 13's mutation outcomes: `COMMITTED` with the document; `ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the original document for a repeated `successionId` with identical bytes, answered **before** any authorization or expiry check, since a device retrying after a lost response finds its proposal already committed and its token already spent; `CONFLICT` with the current certificate when the expected hash is no longer current; `UNAUTHORIZED` for a missing, expired or mismatched token, and for a known `successionId` with different bytes. A timeout is retried with identical bytes and never prepares a second identity.
4. **Activate.** The SDK verifies the returned succession exactly as a peer would (section 3, against the expected previous state it persisted at T11), then writes the T1 header under the new identity, with `accountId` carried from the proposal, and enters `ready`. A succession that does not verify is `SUCCESSION_BINDING` and the device stays `activationPending` with the proposal intact; nothing reverts to the previous identity, because the device never held it.
5. **Bootstrap** (section 5) runs inside ordinary `sync()` calls until done. Its progress and failures are in the sync report; a failed circle does not undo activation or other circles.
6. **Peers** verify the succession on their next sync and rotate (section 3). Until an author rotates, the new device sees that author as waiting, as today. The retired device, on learning the succession, enters `retired` (section 4, "The retired device"), with one addition: it also erases its account seed, since that key is retired.

Crash outcomes (section 11): before T11 commits nothing exists; after T11, restart returns the same proposal; after remote commit but before the client has the succession, the next `completeReplacement` or `sync` gets `ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the document and finishes; a verified committed identity is never replaced by its predecessor.

## 5. Bootstrap without a vault (new text for section 5)

A device that arrives by account recovery holds no vault, so it learns its circles from the host. **Discovery is host-trusted at the level section 15 already grants enrollment**: the host authenticates the account and serves `listCircles(cursor)`: for each circle the account owns, belongs to, or has a pending request in, the `circleId`, the owner's account id, the account's role, the current snapshot, and the account's sharing preference (D3). Pages are bounded; the client persists each page's results before fetching the next and records the cursor.

For each circle the client resolves the owner's chain, verifies the snapshot's validity and authority (section 2: a signature under a key in the owner's verified chain, with the pin rules of section 6), and pins (`circleId`, owner account id) only when the snapshot names this account id as owner, member or, for a pending request, when the host says so (a pending request is host state today and stays so). Discovery is not permission to share: the circle is enabled only if the preference says so, and an absent preference is disabled.

**Currency is host-trusted, and that is a limit, not a guarantee.** A fresh device has no revision checkpoint, so it cannot tell the current snapshot from an older, correctly signed one, nor a preference the user set from one the host asserts. The amendment chooses to trust the host for the currency of bootstrap state and states the consequence in 6.3: a malicious host can roll a fresh device back to a snapshot from before a removal, a departure or a closure, or serve `enabled: true` for a circle the user disabled. What bounds it is the same as for a rolled-back vault today (section 15, item 5): the device can only share with members an owner once admitted, under keys it verifies, and the rollback is undone the moment any member republishes or the owner's next snapshot arrives, since revision rules apply from the first accepted snapshot on. Discovery can also omit a circle, which is withholding and already within the host's power. It cannot forge a snapshot. The original proposal's authority-signed bootstrap checkpoint would not change this: it would be signed by the same party that serves the list, and the amendment does not claim freshness it cannot verify; a product that needs independent freshness evidence needs a source the protocol does not define.

Bootstrap resumes across `sync()` calls, reports `bootstrap: { circles, pending, failed }` in the sync report with per-circle errors, and never erases a circle it already pinned because another failed. The client itself never admits a pending account, re-adds a removed or departed one, reopens a closed circle, or duplicates a member: it writes no membership during bootstrap, it only reads snapshots, and an owner's successor republishes the snapshot it was served, which is why a host-served rollback reaches the owner's next revision only if the host also served it to the owner (the limit above). A successor owner's republication is at the next revision after the served one; the host's compare-and-swap decides, and a host that lies about the current revision is the malicious host of 6.3.

A profile that keeps the vault (D4) may also have the succession install a fresh vault at revision 1 with the discovered entries, so that identity continuity works on the next replacement.

## 6. Security consequences (new text for section 15)

### 6.1 What changes in the attacker table

| Attacker | Can, after this amendment | Still cannot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Holder of an allowed authority key | Sign a succession for any account to keys it holds and inherit everything that account can do: read its **future** traffic from each peer's next rotation; and, when the account owns a circle, sign that circle's next snapshots, admit a member of its choosing, remove members or close the circle, and so read that circle's future traffic through the admitted member. It cannot forge the original owner's signature; it authorizes a successor with the same powers | Read any past traffic; prevent every peer's durable key-change record; sign two successors for one state without producing evidence of equivocation (whether a given peer sees both is another matter, 6.2) |
| Malicious server | Everything above, since the authority key is the host's; present a fabricated login as fresh authentication | Everything in the current table |

Today the same key can only retire identities and close circles. The amendment turns a denial-of-service key into a key-substitution key with ownership powers. The precise guarantee becomes: **a malicious host cannot read past traffic, cannot substitute a key without leaving a durable record on every peer that verified the substitution, and cannot sign two successors for one state without producing evidence that proves it; it can substitute a key, and through an owner it can change membership, and both take effect at each peer's next rotation.** The product must state this guarantee in its own words.

### 6.2 Conditions of adoption

1. **The peer record is mandatory and durable.** Every peer writes the key-change history row in the transaction that accepts a succession (section 3) and the client has no option to drop it; the `accountKeyChanged` event only announces it. Whether and how the product shows it is the product's, but a conforming product MUST make the history discoverable (a notice, a history entry, a settings indicator).
2. **The authority key is custodied.** The operational key that signs successions MUST NOT be a plain environment secret of the application server. It lives in a key-management service or HSM that signs only after the host's fresh-authentication check has passed, with that check and the signing in one audited path. The reset-key list's 30-day lifetime and offline management key apply as today.
3. **Successions are logged per account** by the host and served with the chain (section 7). The log is **evidence, not detection**: two successions for one state are proof of equivocation whenever both are seen together, but a malicious host can serve one branch to one peer and the other to another, and each peer then sees a consistent chain. Detecting a split view requires comparing views across peers or against an independent monitor, which this amendment does not define and does not claim; the log makes misuse provable after the fact and gives a product's own monitoring something to compare. A profile MAY publish the log to a public transparency log, and that is where detection would come from.
4. **Identity continuity remains available in the protocol** (D2), and the model a deployment enables is enforced by its peers (section 2a). A product that can rely on the platform's synced secret store keeps the stronger property; a product that enables account recovery, alone or alongside, says so in its profile and in its user-facing security description, because the weaker enabled model sets the promise.

### 6.3 Limits, stated plainly (additions)

11. A succession is a host assertion signed by the host's authority. Fresh authentication, however strong, is visible to the host alone; peers verify the authority's signature, not the login.
12. Compromise of an operational key now exposes future traffic of every account it is used against until the key is removed from the list and the successions are noticed; the 30-day list lifetime bounds the first, not the second.
13. Previously downloaded plaintext and keys cannot be revoked, by replacement of either kind.
14. Bootstrap state is host-trusted for currency. A fresh device accepts the snapshot and the sharing preference the host serves, so a malicious host can roll it back to a membership from before a removal, a departure or a closure, or re-enable a circle the user disabled; it can only ever share with members an owner once admitted, and the rollback ends at the next republished snapshot. An authenticated write of a preference does not make the value the host later serves trustworthy.
15. Equivocation by the authority is provable, not detected: a peer that is served one consistent branch has no way to know another exists.

## 7. Transport changes (section 13)

Directory, additions:

- `succeedAccount(expectedCertificateHash, newCertificate, newVault?, successionId, authorization)` → `COMMITTED | ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the type 9 document, `CONFLICT` with the current certificate, `UNAUTHORIZED`, `RETRYABLE`.
- `getSuccessions(accountId, afterGeneration)` → the account's successions in order, each with the new certificate, with a link re-attested under a current allowed key wherever its original signer is no longer allowed (section 3, "Authority keys over time"); `resolveIdentities` attaches the chain for an account whose key changed, as it attaches reset receipts today.
- `listCircles(cursor)` → one page of the authenticated account's circles as in section 5, with the sharing preference.
- `setSharingPreference(circleId, enabled)`, authenticated, idempotent; the client calls it on every enable and disable and the vault update when a vault exists. The value the host later serves is host-trusted (6.3, item 14).

Host rules: a retired identity's device is refused every directory mutation, every relay append and every subscription read, by the certificate fence that exists today; the current-device pointer is the one source of "current". The host enforces `successionId` uniqueness and the single-use challenge. Nothing changes on the relay.

Client surface, additions: `prepareReplacement()`, `completeReplacement({ successionId, authorization })`, the `bootstrap` section of the sync report, the states `replacementPrepared` and `activationPending` reused, and the error codes `SUCCESSION_BINDING`, `SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`, `AUTHORIZATION_REQUIRED`, `AUTHORIZATION_EXPIRED`, `SUCCESSION_PENDING`. `restoreAccount` is identity continuity and stays. `resetAccount` stays destructive and separate (D4): it still closes what the identity owned, and it does not share account recovery's continuity code. The profile's `replacement` declaration is validated at `open` like the retention policy, and `inspect()` reports it.

## 8. Conformance and fixture (section 16)

Wire version stays 5; every existing vector stays byte for byte. Added: a type 9 vector signed by the fixture's operational key for the fixture's Alice, with the generation 2 certificate under a new `AK₁`; negatives for a wrong signer, a bad binding (certificate not matching `newCertificateHash`, derived identity not matching `newIdentityId`, generation not previous plus one), a rollback, two successors for one state (`EQUIVOCATION`), and a revoked authority key; state cases for chain following, a gap, and a retired identity attempting to publish. Required tests: scenario 3 and 4 (owner and member replacement) repeated by account recovery with no recovery material; owners and peers missing several successions; a member succession while the owner is offline; removed, departed, pending, disabled and closed circles across a succession; two successions racing for one account (exactly one commits); crashes and lost responses around T11 and the commit; an old device refused after the commit; a succession by an authority key the list no longer allows; discovery pages interrupted and resumed; the lost-device tests of section 16, with the attacker holding the complete old state, showing it opens nothing new and cannot obtain a succession without the product's authentication; on a deployment whose profile enables identity continuity only, a valid succession served by the host being rejected by every peer, the account staying quarantined, and no rotation to the new key; **mixed transitions**: a peer pinned at generation 1 under key A, an identity-continuity replacement to generation 2 the peer never saw, then a succession from generation 2 to key B at 3, accepted on the succession alone; **an owner's succession**: snapshots signed by the retired owner key still verify, the successor republishes at the next revision in its first bootstrap sync, the host refuses a publication under the retired key, and a member device bootstrapping in the window between verifies the retired-key snapshot; **a long-offline peer** returning across an authority-key rotation, verifying a chain whose first link is re-attested, and the same peer refusing an unseen link under a revoked key with no re-attestation; **a crash between the acceptance transaction and the event**, after which the key-change history row is present and the succession is a duplicate; and **the bootstrap limit as a test**: a host serving a fresh device a snapshot from before a removal, showing the device shares only with once-admitted members and corrects itself at the next republished snapshot.

## 10. What revision 2 changed, against the first review

1. The attacker table and D1 say that the authority can inherit an owner's membership powers, not only an account's traffic; the "cannot admit a member or change a snapshot" claim is withdrawn.
2. Snapshots and manifests are verified under the chain key that signed them; validity, authority and currency are three questions; only the current key advances a revision; an owner's successor republishes in its first bootstrap sync.
3. A succession's previous generation need only be at least the pinned one under the same identity, so identity-continuity activations between successions need no extra evidence; the chain is contiguous only between successions; the mixed-transition test is added.
4. Bootstrap currency is declared host-trusted, with rollback and preference substitution stated as limits 14 and 15; the "never resurrects" sentence now describes what the client writes, not what the host can do.
5. The per-account log is evidence, not detection; split-view detection is named as out of scope; the comparison to other products is removed and the precise guarantee stated instead.
6. Authority keys over time: ordinary rotation is handled by re-attestation under a current key, as receipts are today; compromise forbids re-attestation; already-verified links stay verified.
7. The key-change record is a row written in the acceptance transaction; the event only announces it.
