# Amendment A1: account replacement authorized by the host's signing authority

Status: **draft for review, revision 4, not adopted; design conditionally approved by the third review**. Nothing in the SDK, the hosts or the fixture implements it. Revisions 2, 3 and 4 answer the three reviews in turn; section 10 lists every change. Approval of the design is not the trust choice: a product still records D1 to D5, and `replacement` is `continuity`, `recovery` or `both` by its decision alone. It proposes protocol revision 9 on wire version 5. Section numbers refer to `spec/Kavach-v1.md` at revision 8. Where this document and the specification disagree today, the specification wins; if the amendment is adopted, its text replaces the sections it names.

## 0. What this changes, in one paragraph

Today a replacement device proves it is the same account by holding the account key, recovered through the platform's synced secret store or the recovery code (section 4, "Replacing the device"). This amendment adds a second way: a new device with **no secret from the old one** generates fresh account and device keys, and the host's signing authority, after the product's fresh authentication, signs a **succession** that binds the account to the new keys. Peers verify the succession and rotate, exactly as they do for a device change today; nobody approves anything; circle ids, membership, pending joins, ownership and closures are unchanged; the old device is fenced; the new device receives no historical epoch key, so a succession by itself enables no decryption of ciphertext encrypted under earlier epochs. The price is stated in section 6 and is the reason this is a decision and not a patch: an authority that can sign successions can bind any account to keys of its choosing, and through an owner's account it inherits that owner's power over membership.

## 2. Identity model

### 2a. The two replacement models

| Model | How the new device proves it is the account | Who must be trusted | Name in this document |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Host-independent identity continuity** | It holds the account key, recovered through the platform's synced secret store or the recovery code (section 4, "Replacing the device", unchanged) | Nobody beyond first contact: the host cannot rebind a key | identity continuity |
| **Convenient account recovery** | It holds nothing; the host's authority signs a succession after the product's fresh authentication (sections 3 and 4 below) | The host's signing authority, for every account's future traffic and, through owners, for every circle's membership | account recovery |

A profile declares which models it enables: `replacement: "continuity" | "recovery" | "both"`. The declaration is a property of the app build, decided by the product and never by a user: it is compiled into every client, it has no runtime setter, no per-account form and no server-delivered value, and it changes only by shipping a new app version, as the pinned management key does. What a user ever sees is the consequence (what a replacement asks of them, and the notice when a contact's key changes), never a choice. **The declaration is enforced by the peers who verify, not only by the device being replaced.** A client whose profile does not enable account recovery rejects every type 9 document as `UNSUPPORTED_TYPE`, whoever signed it, keeps the account's key quarantined as `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED` exactly as it does today for a bare directory key change, and ignores the chain the directory attaches. That is what makes identity continuity host-independent in fact: on such a deployment a host that tries to rebind a key obtains nothing from any client. A client that enables account recovery verifies successions by section 3. A product that enables both has the convenience of both and the guarantee of account recovery, since every account on it can be rebound by the host; its profile states the enabled models and the resulting promise in one sentence, so nobody reads the stronger guarantee into a deployment that allows the weaker model.


The protocol gains one definition and changes none on the wire.

- **`accountId`** is the `identityId` an account had at enrollment: `H(C("identity") || B(accountName) || AK₀)`, where `AK₀` is its first account key. It never changes. Every document that names an account keeps naming it by `accountId`: the owner and members of a snapshot (type 2), the author and rows of a manifest (type 3), and the vault header's binding. A reset receipt (type 7) names the account's **current** identity as its old identity and the receipt's effects are applied to the account id reached through the chain (section 3c).
- **`identityId`** keeps its definition, `H(C("identity") || B(accountName) || AK)`, and is now the identity of the account's **current key**. For an account never replaced by authority, `accountId = identityId`, which is every account that exists today.
- A **succession chain** for an account is the ordered list of verified successions from `accountId` to its current `identityId`. Resolving an account means following its chain to the current key and certificate. The chain is empty for an account that has never succeeded.
- `deviceGeneration` is the **account version**. It is already monotonic per account and already advances by exactly one at every activation under compare-and-swap on the host. A succession advances it by one, like a device change under the same key. There is no second version counter.

Consequences for existing rules:

- Section 4, "First contact and pinning": a different `AK` for a pinned account remains `ACCOUNT_KEY_CHANGED` and remains quarantined, until either a reset receipt (section 12, as today) or a verified succession (section 4 below) is processed. The rule "never on an unsigned directory change" stands.
- Section 6, snapshots: members and the owner are account ids. "Resolving members MUST reject two different account keys for one account name" becomes "two different current keys for one account id". A member's succession never changes a snapshot and the owner does not republish for it. An owner's succession does: the successor's first act in bootstrap (section 5) is to republish the current snapshot at the next revision under its new key, so the circle has a snapshot under the current owner key within one sync. Three questions are kept apart for every snapshot (the same split applies to manifests below): **validity**, the signature verifies under its signer; **authority**, that signer is a key in the owner's verified chain, current or retired; **currency**, whether the snapshot may govern membership and sharing now. The exact rule has two cases. **Fresh pin** (a circle the device has no snapshot for, as in bootstrap): a valid snapshot under any key in the owner's chain, current or retired, establishes the circle's state. **Established circle**: before the peer has accepted an owner succession, the rules of section 6 apply unchanged; after it, only a snapshot signed by the owner's current key advances the revision, and a later-arriving snapshot signed by a retired owner key, even with a higher revision, is held and not applied; the peer keeps its last verified snapshot and reports its age, as it does for a withheld snapshot today, until the successor's republication (its first bootstrap sync) arrives. The host refuses a publication signed by a retired key. Retired owner keys are retained in the chain for as long as a snapshot they signed may still be verified.
- Section 7, audience: an audience entry is (`accountId`, `certificateHash`, `DEK.public`) for the account's current device under its current key, found through the chain. Deduplication and the 100-recipient limit are by account id, as today. A changed key with a verified succession is a rotation trigger (an accepted recipient key change), not a security error.
- Section 8, manifests: `authorIdentityId` and `recipientIdentityId` are account ids. The reader verifies the author's certificate under the chain key that signed it, which may be a retired key for an epoch published before a succession, and checks that the certificate's derived identity is the chain identity of that generation and its stream id matches. Only the author's current key and device may publish a new epoch; a manifest from a retired device is history only, as a superseded device's records are today (section 9, "Verification"). The reader's own row must name the reader's account id and its current certificate hash.
- The relay keys streams by `streamId`, which already changes with every device; nothing in section 10 changes.

## 3. The succession document

A new signed document, type 9, label `succession-sig`, signed by an operational key allowed by the current reset-key list (section 12). It is distinct from the reset receipt: a receipt retires an identity and closes what it owned; a succession continues everything.

```
body = u8(5) || u8(9) || B(accountName) || accountId[32]
       || previousIdentityId[32] || newIdentityId[32]
       || previousGeneration:u64 || newGeneration:u64
       || newCertificateHash[32] || successionId[16] || issuedAt:u64 || authorityKeyId[16]
wire = body || Ed25519(authorityKey, C("succession-sig") || body)
```

Rules: `newGeneration = previousGeneration + 1`; previous and new identity differ; `successionId` is random, 16 bytes, nonzero, and unique per account; `issuedAt` is diagnostic. Wire length is 251 bytes plus the name length: two type bytes, the name with its length byte, three 32-byte identifiers, two 8-byte generations, the 32-byte certificate hash, the 16-byte succession id, the 8-byte time, the 16-byte key id and the 64-byte signature. The fixture asserts the encoded length, as it does for every other type.

The document has two parts, and every rule below names which it means. The **transition** is (`accountName`, `accountId`, `successionId`, `previousIdentityId`, `newIdentityId`, `previousGeneration`, `newGeneration`, `newCertificateHash`): what happened to the account. The **attestation** is (`authorityKeyId`, `issuedAt`, signature): who vouched for it and when. Two documents with the same transition are the **same succession** whatever their attestations; a re-attestation (below) is a new attestation of an unchanged transition. Two documents with different transitions from the same previous state are two successors for one state: equivocation. The peer persists the transition tuple, never a hash of the whole body, and compares transitions field by field.

**Verification by a peer**, for a received succession document of account `accountId`, **in this order**, because a second attestation of an accepted transition must be recognised before the pin checks would reject it, and a conflicting transition from a retired identity must be recorded as equivocation rather than discarded:

- **Order 1, authenticate the attestation:** step 1 below. A document that fails it is `BAD_SIGNATURE` or a key-list error and is discarded; nothing about it is recorded.
- **Order 2, compare the transition with recorded transitions.** Equal to a recorded transition, field by field: a `DUPLICATE`, accepted as the same succession whatever its attestation, with no further check; this is also how a transition verified long ago stays recognised after its signing key has left the list. Same (`accountId`, `previousIdentityId`, `previousGeneration`) as a recorded transition but a different transition: `EQUIVOCATION`, recorded and quarantining the account, even if the previous identity is retired. Otherwise the transition is new.
- **Order 3, apply steps 2 to 5 below to the new transition.**

1. A fresh reset-key list (section 12 rules, including `RESET_KEYS_REQUIRED` and `RESET_CLOCK_UNTRUSTED`), an allowed `authorityKeyId`, a valid signature under that key.
2. `accountName` and `accountId` match the local pin for that account.
3. `previousIdentityId` equals the account's current pinned identity, and `previousGeneration` is at least the pinned generation. The two may differ by identity-continuity activations the peer has not seen: under one key a higher generation with gaps is legal today (section 4, "Generation rules"), and a succession from such a generation is accepted on the same terms, with no further certificate evidence. Between successions the chain is contiguous: a succession's previous identity must be the identity the prior succession produced. A previous generation below the pinned one is `ROLLBACK`. Two validly attested documents whose transitions differ but share (`accountId`, `previousIdentityId`, `previousGeneration`) are `EQUIVOCATION`, which quarantines the account: the authority signed two successors for one state. Two documents with the same transition are one succession, and the second is a duplicate (`DUPLICATE`), whatever its attestation.
4. A verified type 1 certificate whose body hash is `newCertificateHash`, signed by the new `AK`, carrying `accountName`, `deviceGeneration = newGeneration`, a new `deviceId`, and whose derived identity is `newIdentityId` (`SUCCESSION_BINDING` otherwise, a new code in the trust family).
5. Neither the previous nor the new identity is in the account's **retired set**: every identity the chain has passed through, every identity a reset receipt retired, and the account id itself once reset (section 3c). A previous identity in the set is `SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`; a new identity in the set is also `SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`, and it is the case that matters most: A → B → A would otherwise satisfy every other check with a certificate signed by a key a retired device still holds. Retirement is permanent.

On acceptance, in one transaction: pin the new `AK`, identity, generation and certificate hash for the account; retire the previous identity (its certificates verify nothing new; its stream is history only); increment `desiredRevision` (section 7); record the transition tuple and add the previous identity to the retired set, so a document with the same transition is a no-op and a document with a known `successionId` and a different transition is rejected (`EQUIVOCATION`); and **write a key-change history row** (`accountId`, previous and new identity, generation, `successionId`, accepted-at) in the same transaction. The row is the notice: it is durable, it is read through `inspect()` and a `keyChanges(accountId?)` query, and a product that shows key changes reads it. After commit, `accountKeyChanged { accountId, accountName, reason: "succession", generation }` tells a listener that the history changed; a crash between commit and event loses the event and nothing else, since the row is already there. The row is not optional and the client never suppresses it; what the product shows is the product's decision (section 6).

**Chains.** A peer that missed successions receives them in order from the directory (section 7 below) and verifies each against the state the previous one produced. A peer applies no succession out of order and never skips one: a gap is `METADATA_REQUIRED`, and the peer pauses sharing to that account until the chain is complete, as it does today for a missing certificate.

**Authority keys over time.** Step 1 requires a signer the current list allows, and a link signed long ago may carry a key id the list has since dropped. Three cases are kept apart. *Rotation*: the key was retired in the ordinary course; the host re-attests the link under a current allowed key with the identical transition, as section 12 already permits for receipts, and serves the re-attestation in the chain; the peer verifies the re-attestation and never the original. *Compromise*: the key was revoked because it was misused; the host MUST NOT re-attest links it cannot vouch for, and a peer that is served an unseen link under a revoked key without a re-attestation reports `RESET_KEY_REVOKED` and keeps the account quarantined; which links a host vouches for after a compromise is a host duty the protocol states and cannot verify. *History*: a link a peer verified before the key was dropped stays verified, as a committed reset stays committed today. `issuedAt` decides none of this; it is diagnostic.

### 3c. Destructive reset after a succession (changes to section 12)

A reset remains the one way to abandon an account's relationships, and it must compose with chains. After `A → B`, the account id is `A`, the current identity is `B`, and `A` is retired as an encryption identity.

- **What the receipt binds.** The receipt's `oldIdentityId` is the account's **current** identity (`B`), and its `accountName` is the account's. A receipt naming a retired identity as old is `RESET_BINDING`. Peers resolve the receipt's old identity through the chain to the account id (`A`), and every effect below applies to that account id. The host checks the same binding before signing.
- **What becomes unusable.** On a validated receipt a peer retires every identity in the account's chain, from the account id to the current identity, and marks the account id itself retired. No later succession may name any of them as previous or new (section 3, step 5), so **no succession can restore the discarded relationships**: a reset is final for that account id.
- **The new identity is a new account root.** `newIdentityId` is a new account id with an empty chain, no circles and, as today, a fresh vault when the profile uses one. It is a different account to the protocol; the host may map the same login to it.
- **What closes.** Peers treat every circle owned by the reset account id as closed and stop sharing to that account id, exactly as today; an owner that had succeeded is recognised through its account id, which is what snapshots name, so a succession before the reset changes nothing here.
- **Order.** A reset and a succession for one account cannot both commit against one current state: the host's compare-and-swap on the current certificate orders them, and a peer that sees both for one previous state treats the pair as `EQUIVOCATION`, since the authority vouched for two futures.

## 4. Replacing the account keys (new text for section 4)

The existing procedure, "Replacing the device", is **identity continuity**, and its security semantics are unchanged. Its implementation changes in one respect once chains exist: a device restoring under the current key `B` of an account that has succeeded cannot derive the account id from `B`. It resolves the account through the directory, verifies the served chain link by link (section 3), takes the account id from the chain's root, carries it into its T1 header, and advances the generation under `B`; the vault it opens is the one the succession installed. Every account reference is resolved through the chain, never derived from the current key alone. This is **account recovery**. A profile enables one or both (section 2a); the SDK refuses to prepare a replacement by a model the profile has not enabled, and peers refuse to verify one.

1. **Prepare** (`prepareReplacement()`, T11). On a fresh device, authenticated to the host as the account by the product's own login, the SDK resolves the account's current certificate and generation `g` through the directory, generates a fresh `accountSeed`, `deviceSeed`, `deviceId` and, when the profile uses a vault, a fresh `recoveryKey`, builds the certificate at generation `g + 1` under the new `AK`, and persists the roots, the exact certificate bytes, a random `successionId`, the expected current certificate hash and `g` in the secret store and the store, before returning. It returns the **proposal**: `{ successionId, accountName, accountId, expectedCertificateHash, expectedGeneration, certificate }`, all public. A second call on the same device returns the same pending proposal. The remote account is untouched.
2. **Authorize** (outside the SDK). The product performs its fresh authentication and asks the host to authorize the proposal. The host binds the authorization to the proposal's hash, the account, the expected generation and a single-use challenge with an expiry, and returns an opaque token. A long-lived session alone MUST NOT obtain one. How the host authenticates is the product's; the protocol sees only the token.
3. **Commit** (`completeReplacement({ successionId, authorization })`). The SDK calls `succeedAccount(expectedCertificateHash, certificate, vault?, successionId, authorization)`. The host, atomically: checks the authorization against the proposal and the challenge; compares and swaps the account's current certificate hash; stores the new certificate; signs the type 9 succession under the active operational key; moves the current-device pointer; fences the previous device from every write and from the relay; records the succession in the account's log; and returns the succession. Outcomes follow section 13's mutation outcomes: `COMMITTED` with the document; `ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the original document for a repeated `successionId` with identical bytes, answered **before** any authorization or expiry check, since a device retrying after a lost response finds its proposal already committed and its token already spent; `CONFLICT` with the current certificate when the expected hash is no longer current; `UNAUTHORIZED` for a missing, expired or mismatched token, and for a known `successionId` with different bytes. A timeout is retried with identical bytes and never prepares a second identity.
4. **Activate.** The SDK verifies the returned succession exactly as a peer would (section 3, against the expected previous state it persisted at T11), then writes the T1 header under the new identity, with `accountId` carried from the proposal, and enters `ready`. A succession that does not verify is `SUCCESSION_BINDING` and the device stays `activationPending` with the proposal intact; nothing reverts to the previous identity, because the device never held it.

   **Three outcomes of a commit that did not plainly succeed** are kept apart, and only one of them discards the proposal. *Unknown*: a timeout or lost response; the SDK retries the identical call, or asks the chain (`getSuccessions`) whether its `successionId` is committed, and never prepares another identity. *Committed elsewhere*: `ALREADY_COMMITTED` with our own `successionId`; the SDK finishes from the returned document. *Definitive conflict*: `CONFLICT`, the current certificate has moved, and the chain shows a succession or an activation that is not ours (a legitimate replacement on another device, say). The proposal can never commit: the SDK marks it stale in the same transaction that records the conflict, discards its keys, which were never activated anywhere, reports `REPLACEMENT_CONFLICT` with the current generation, and the next `prepareReplacement()` prepares against the current state. The product then obtains fresh authorization; the SDK never retries a replacement on its own, and the two calls remain the whole API.
5. **Bootstrap** (section 5) runs inside ordinary `sync()` calls until done. Its progress and failures are in the sync report; a failed circle does not undo activation or other circles.
6. **Peers** verify the succession on their next sync and rotate (section 3). Until an author rotates, the new device sees that author as waiting, as today. The retired device, on learning the succession, enters `retired` (section 4, "The retired device"), with one addition: it also erases its account seed, since that key is retired.

Crash outcomes (section 11): before T11 commits nothing exists; after T11, restart returns the same proposal; after remote commit but before the client has the succession, the next `completeReplacement` or `sync` gets `ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the document and finishes; a verified committed identity is never replaced by its predecessor.

## 5. Bootstrap without a vault (new text for section 5)

A device that arrives by account recovery holds no vault, so it learns its circles from the host. **Discovery is host-trusted at the level section 15 already grants enrollment**: the host authenticates the account and serves `listCircles(cursor)`: for each circle the account owns, belongs to, or has a pending request in, the `circleId`, the owner's account id, the account's role, the current snapshot, and the account's sharing preference (D3). Pages are bounded; the client persists each page's results before fetching the next and records the cursor.

For each circle the client resolves the owner's chain, verifies the snapshot's validity and authority (section 2: a signature under a key in the owner's verified chain, with the pin rules of section 6), and pins (`circleId`, owner account id) only when the snapshot names this account id as owner, member or, for a pending request, when the host says so (a pending request is host state today and stays so). Discovery is not permission to share: the circle is enabled only if the preference says so, and an absent preference is disabled.

**Currency is host-trusted, and that is a limit, not a guarantee.** A fresh device has no revision checkpoint, so it cannot tell the current snapshot from an older, correctly signed one, nor a preference the user set from one the host asserts. The amendment chooses to trust the host for the currency of bootstrap state and states the consequence in 6.3: a malicious host can roll a fresh device back to a snapshot from before a removal, a departure or a closure, or serve `enabled: true` for a circle the user disabled. What bounds it is the same as for a rolled-back vault today (section 15, item 5): the device can only share with members an owner once admitted, under keys it verifies, and the bound does not hold against the combined attacker of 6.1, who can replace the owner and admit anyone. Rollback may persist for as long as the host withholds newer state; receiving an acceptable newer snapshot corrects it, and a conflicting snapshot at a revision the device already holds quarantines the circle instead, as the revision rules of section 6 require. Discovery can also omit a circle, which is withholding and already within the host's power. It cannot forge a snapshot. The original proposal's authority-signed bootstrap checkpoint would not change this: it would be signed by the same party that serves the list, and the amendment does not claim freshness it cannot verify; a product that needs independent freshness evidence needs a source the protocol does not define.

Bootstrap resumes across `sync()` calls, reports `bootstrap: { circles, pending, failed }` in the sync report with per-circle errors, and never erases a circle it already pinned because another failed. The client itself never admits a pending account, re-adds a removed or departed one, reopens a closed circle, or duplicates a member: it writes no membership during bootstrap, it only reads snapshots, and an owner's successor republishes the snapshot it was served, which is why a host-served rollback reaches the owner's next revision only if the host also served it to the owner (the limit above), and persists while the host withholds the newer state. A successor owner's republication is at the next revision after the served one; the host's compare-and-swap decides, and a host that lies about the current revision is the malicious host of 6.3.

A profile that keeps the vault (D4) may also have the succession install a fresh vault at revision 1 with the discovered entries, so that identity continuity works on the next replacement.

## 6. Security consequences (new text for section 15)

### 6.1 What changes in the attacker table

| Attacker | Can, after this amendment | Still cannot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Holder of an allowed authority key | Sign a succession for any account to keys it holds and inherit everything that account can do: read its **future** traffic from each peer's next rotation; and, when the account owns a circle, sign that circle's next snapshots, admit a member of its choosing, remove members or close the circle, and so read that circle's future traffic through the admitted member. It cannot forge the original owner's signature; it authorizes a successor with the same powers | Obtain any historical epoch key, or by itself decrypt ciphertext encrypted only under earlier epochs; prevent every peer's durable key-change record; sign two successors for one state without producing evidence of equivocation (whether a given peer sees both is another matter, 6.2) |
| Malicious server | Everything above, since the authority key is the host's; present a fabricated login as fresh authentication | Everything in the current table |

Today the same key can only retire identities and close circles. The amendment turns a denial-of-service key into a key-substitution key with ownership powers. The precise guarantee becomes: **a succession transfers no historical epoch key and by itself enables no decryption of ciphertext encrypted only under earlier epochs; a malicious host cannot substitute a key without leaving a durable record on every peer that verified the substitution, and cannot sign two successors for one state without producing evidence that proves it; it can substitute a key, and through an owner it can change membership, and both take effect at each peer's next rotation.** What an application resends under a new epoch is outside this guarantee; that is the deferred stale-record question (D5). The product must state this guarantee in its own words.

### 6.2 Conditions of adoption

1. **The peer record is mandatory and durable.** Every peer writes the key-change history row in the transaction that accepts a succession (section 3) and the client has no option to drop it; the `accountKeyChanged` event only announces it. Whether and how the product shows it is the product's, but a conforming product MUST make the history discoverable (a notice, a history entry, a settings indicator).
2. **The authority key is custodied.** The operational key that signs successions MUST NOT be a plain environment secret of the application server. It lives in a key-management service or HSM that signs only after the host's fresh-authentication check has passed, with that check and the signing in one audited path. The reset-key list's 30-day lifetime and offline management key apply as today.
3. **Successions are logged per account** by the host and served with the chain (section 7). The log is **evidence, not detection**: two successions for one state are proof of equivocation whenever both are seen together, but a malicious host can serve one branch to one peer and the other to another, and each peer then sees a consistent chain. Detecting a split view requires comparing views across peers or against an independent monitor, which this amendment does not define and does not claim; the log makes misuse provable after the fact and gives a product's own monitoring something to compare. A profile MAY publish the log to a public transparency log, and that is where detection would come from.
4. **Identity continuity remains available in the protocol** (D2), and the model a deployment enables is enforced by its peers (section 2a). A product that can rely on the platform's synced secret store keeps the stronger property; a product that enables account recovery, alone or alongside, says so in its profile and in its user-facing security description, because the weaker enabled model sets the promise.

### 6.3 Limits, stated plainly (additions)

11. A succession is a host assertion signed by the host's authority. Fresh authentication, however strong, is visible to the host alone; peers verify the authority's signature, not the login.
12. Compromise of an operational key now exposes future traffic of every account it is used against until the key is removed from the list and the successions are noticed; the 30-day list lifetime bounds the first, not the second.
13. Previously downloaded plaintext and keys cannot be revoked, by replacement of either kind.
14. Bootstrap state is host-trusted for currency. A fresh device accepts the snapshot and the sharing preference the host serves, so a malicious host can roll it back to a membership from before a removal, a departure or a closure, or re-enable a circle the user disabled. Rollback may persist while the host withholds newer state; an acceptable newer snapshot corrects it, and a conflicting one quarantines the circle. The device can only share with members an owner once admitted, which does not bound an attacker who can also replace the owner. An authenticated write of a preference does not make the value the host later serves trustworthy.
15. Equivocation by the authority is provable, not detected: a peer that is served one consistent branch has no way to know another exists.

## 7. Transport changes (section 13)

Directory, additions:

- `succeedAccount(expectedCertificateHash, newCertificate, newVault?, successionId, authorization)` → `COMMITTED | ALREADY_COMMITTED` with the type 9 document, `CONFLICT` with the current certificate, `UNAUTHORIZED`, `RETRYABLE`.
- `getSuccessions(accountId, afterGeneration, limit)` → one page of the account's successions in generation order, each with the new certificate, with a link re-attested under a current allowed key wherever its original signer is no longer allowed (section 3, "Authority keys over time"), and `nextGeneration` when more remain. The client processes a page inside the sync budget (section 13, `catchUp`), persists the chain it has verified so far, and continues on the next sync, as it does for records; a chain the host cannot finish serving leaves the account at `METADATA_REQUIRED`, never in a half-applied state. `resolveIdentities` attaches the first page for an account whose key changed, as it attaches reset receipts today.
- `listCircles(cursor)` → one page of the authenticated account's circles as in section 5, with the sharing preference.
- `setSharingPreference(circleId, enabled, expectedRevision)`, authenticated, compare-and-swap on a per-circle preference revision the host keeps, returning the new revision or `CONFLICT` with the current value and revision. Idempotence alone would let a delayed enable overwrite a later disable with an honest host; the revision orders them. The client persists the pending change with the revision it expects (T9, as a vault update), applies it locally in the same transaction, so a disable stops sharing at once whether or not the host has heard of it, and sync retries it until it lands or conflicts; on `CONFLICT` the local value wins and is written again at the served revision, since the user's latest action is the one the device holds. Reconciliation order on any read: the device's own last action, then the host flag at the last confirmed revision, then the vault where one exists. The value the host later serves is host-trusted (6.3, item 14). `listCircles` returns the preference with its revision.

Host rules: a retired identity's device is refused every directory mutation, every relay append and every subscription read, by the certificate fence that exists today; the current-device pointer is the one source of "current". The host enforces `successionId` uniqueness and the single-use challenge. Nothing changes on the relay.

Client surface, additions: `prepareReplacement()`, `completeReplacement({ successionId, authorization })`, the `bootstrap` section of the sync report, the states `replacementPrepared` and `activationPending` reused, and the error codes `SUCCESSION_BINDING`, `SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`, `AUTHORIZATION_REQUIRED`, `AUTHORIZATION_EXPIRED`, `SUCCESSION_PENDING`, `REPLACEMENT_CONFLICT`. `restoreAccount` is identity continuity and stays. `resetAccount` stays destructive and separate (D4), with the rules of 3c: it retires the whole chain and the account id, closes what the account id owned, starts a new account root, and shares none of account recovery's continuity code. The profile's `replacement` declaration is validated at `open` like the retention policy, and `inspect()` reports it.

## 10. What each revision changed, against its review

### Revision 2, against the first review

1. The attacker table and D1 say that the authority can inherit an owner's membership powers, not only an account's traffic; the "cannot admit a member or change a snapshot" claim is withdrawn.
2. Snapshots and manifests are verified under the chain key that signed them; validity, authority and currency are three questions; only the current key advances a revision; an owner's successor republishes in its first bootstrap sync.
3. A succession's previous generation need only be at least the pinned one under the same identity, so identity-continuity activations between successions need no extra evidence; the chain is contiguous only between successions; the mixed-transition test is added.
4. Bootstrap currency is declared host-trusted, with rollback and preference substitution stated as limits 14 and 15; the "never resurrects" sentence now describes what the client writes, not what the host can do.
5. The per-account log is evidence, not detection; split-view detection is named as out of scope; the comparison to other products is removed and the precise guarantee stated instead.
6. Authority keys over time: ordinary rotation is handled by re-attestation under a current key, as receipts are today; compromise forbids re-attestation; already-verified links stay verified.
7. The key-change record is a row written in the acceptance transaction; the event only announces it.

### Revision 3, against the second review

1. A succession whose new identity is in the account's retired set is `SUPERSEDED_IDENTITY`; retirement is permanent; the A → B → A test is added.
2. The document is a transition plus an attestation; equivocation compares transitions, duplicates compare transitions, re-attestation changes only the attestation; the persisted tuple is named in full.
3. Section 3c specifies destructive reset after a succession: the receipt names the current identity, effects apply to the account id through the chain, the whole chain and the account id retire, the new identity is a new root, no succession can restore the old account, and host ordering against a concurrent succession is stated; the succession, reset, recovery test is added.
4. Historical snapshots: a fresh pin accepts any chain key; an established circle after an accepted owner succession advances only under the current key and holds a late retired-key snapshot until republication; the waiting behaviour is documented and tested.
5. Rollback may persist while the host withholds, an acceptable newer snapshot corrects and a conflicting one quarantines; the once-admitted bound is said not to hold against the combined attacker.
6. "Cannot read any past traffic" is replaced everywhere by the cryptographic guarantee: no historical epoch key is transferred and no decryption under earlier epochs is enabled by the succession itself; application resends are the deferred stale-record question.

### Revision 4, against the third review

1. Sharing preferences carry a per-circle revision with compare-and-swap; the pending change is persisted and applied locally first, so a disable takes effect before the host hears of it; reconciliation order is stated; tests for reordering, a lost response and a restart.
2. A commit that did not plainly succeed is one of three cases: unknown (retry or look the succession id up), committed elsewhere (finish), or definitive conflict (`REPLACEMENT_CONFLICT`: the proposal is marked stale, its never-activated keys discarded, and the next prepare targets the current state); the two-call API is unchanged and the SDK never retries on its own.
3. Verification is ordered: authenticate the attestation, compare the transition with recorded ones (duplicate or equivocation), then pin and retirement checks for a new transition; a recorded transition stays recognised after its key leaves the list.
4. The succession wire length is 251 bytes plus the name; the fixture asserts it.
5. The composition test of both models is added, and identity continuity's implementation is stated to resolve the account id through the verified chain, never from the current key alone.
6. `getSuccessions` is paged and bounded and processed within the sync budget.
